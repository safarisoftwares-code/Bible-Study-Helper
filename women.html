What this book is — and isn't

  • It is a devotional celebration of women of faith, courage, service, prayer and prophecy in Scripture.
  • It is not the Bible. Scripture is the authority; this book is a fallible human aid to reading it.
  • It is not exhaustive. Scripture records well over a hundred women. This book highlights a selection. Many are not mentioned here.
  • It is not a position paper. It does not address contested questions about roles in public worship. Those deserve their own careful, prayerful study — in the Bible, with your congregation.
  • It quotes by reference, not by replacement. Every verse cited links to the full text in the Safari Reference Bible.

Read the Bible first. Read this second. Where they differ, the Bible wins.

Women of Faith — A Devotional Catalog

1. Prophetesses

MiriamExodus 15:20 ↗

Moses' sister — the one who watched the basket in the reeds and offered to find a nurse for the baby (Exodus 2:4–8). When the sea split and Israel crossed, she took a timbrel and led the women in song: "Sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously" (Exodus 15:21). Hers is the first recorded worship chorus in Scripture. Later she and Aaron spoke against Moses and were rebuked — but her prophetic voice, her song, and her leadership of Israel's women stand as a memorial.

DeborahJudges 4–5 ↗

Israel's only female judge, and one of its boldest. She held court under a palm tree between Ramah and Bethel, and the people came to her for judgment. When God commanded Barak to muster an army against Sisera, Barak refused to go without her — so she went, and told him plainly that the honour of the victory would go to a woman instead. She was right: Sisera fell to Jael's tent-peg, and Deborah sang the song of victory that is still in our Bibles (Judges 5). Her leadership was not tolerated; it was sought out. And when the battle was won, she gave the glory to God.

Also appears in: Leaders & Judges · Women of Courage & Justice
Huldah2 Kings 22:14 ↗

When King Josiah's workmen found the lost Book of the Law in the temple, the king tore his clothes and sent his officials to inquire of the LORD. They went to Huldah, a prophetess living in Jerusalem. She spoke the word of the LORD: judgment was coming on the nation, but not in Josiah's day, because his heart was tender. Her words launched one of the greatest reforms in Israel's history (2 Kings 23). A woman's voice from a quiet room steered a kingdom.

AnnaLuke 2:36 ↗

A prophetess of the tribe of Asher, widowed after seven years of marriage and now eighty-four. She never left the temple, serving God with fasting and prayer night and day. When Mary and Joseph brought the infant Jesus to be presented, Anna came up at that very hour, gave thanks to God, and spoke of the child to all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem (Luke 2:38). A lifetime of prayer met the Messiah in a single moment — and she spent the rest of it telling people.

Also appears in: Women of Exceptional Faith · Women Who Prayed
Philip's four daughtersActs 21:9 ↗

Unnamed, unmarried, and prophesying. Luke mentions them in a single verse, but they are a quiet sign of Pentecost fulfilled: "your sons and your daughters shall prophesy" (Acts 2:17). Their father was an evangelist; their home, in Caesarea, was the place where Paul stayed on his last journey to Jerusalem. Their gift is recorded without comment, regulation, or apology — the Spirit had been poured out on daughters as well as sons.

2. Leaders & Judges

EstherEsther 4:16 ↗

An orphan raised by her cousin Mordecai, taken into the harem of a Persian king, and made queen of an empire that did not know her God. When Haman's decree threatened to destroy the Jews, Mordecai sent her a message: perhaps you were made queen for such a time as this. She called for a three-day fast, dressed in her royal robes, and walked into the king's inner court — an uninvited woman facing a king with the power to kill her. "If I perish, I perish" (Esther 4:16). She did not perish; the king held out his sceptre, and the plot against her people collapsed. Her story is read at Purim to this day.

Also appears in: Women Who Saved Lives · Women of Courage & Justice
JaelJudges 4:21 ↗

When Sisera fled the battlefield on foot, he took refuge in the tent of Heber the Kenite — and Heber's wife Jael met him. She gave him milk, covered him, and waited until he slept. Then she took a tent-peg and a hammer and ended the war with the tools she had in her hand. Deborah's song calls her "most blessed of women" (Judges 5:24). Sometimes courage is not a sword; sometimes it is a tent-peg and a still moment.

Also appears in: Women of Courage & Justice
Zelophehad's daughtersNumbers 27:1–11 ↗

Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah and Tirzah — five sisters who came to Moses with a case. Their father had died in the wilderness leaving no sons, and the inheritance law of the day gave nothing to daughters. They stood before Moses, Eleazar the priest, and the whole congregation and asked for a possession among their father's brothers. Moses brought the case to the LORD — and God said: "The daughters of Zelophehad are right." The law was changed, and their inheritance was secured (Numbers 27:7; 36). They were not rebels; they trusted God's justice enough to ask.

Also appears in: Women of Courage & Justice
Daughters of ShallumNehemiah 3:12 ↗

Nehemiah lists the builders of Jerusalem's wall by name — and among them, "Shallum the son of Hallohesh, ruler of half the district of Jerusalem, he and his daughters" (Nehemiah 3:12). They built alongside the men, their names in the record, their work in the stones. When God's city was rebuilt, women were among the builders. It is a small verse that has strengthened generations of women who were told building was not their work.

3. Women Who Saved Lives

Shiphrah & PuahExodus 1:17 ↗

Two Hebrew midwives named by Pharaoh himself. He commanded them: when you serve the Hebrew women, kill the boys. But they feared God and did not do as Pharaoh commanded — they let the boys live. When Pharaoh demanded an explanation, they answered that the Hebrew women were vigorous and gave birth before the midwives arrived. God dealt well with them and gave them families (Exodus 1:20–21). Two women with no political power refused an empire's order — and Israel was preserved.

Also appears in: Women of Courage & Justice
JochebedExodus 2:2 ↗

A Levite woman who hid her newborn son for three months because he was beautiful — and when she could hide him no longer, she built a basket of bulrushes, sealed it with pitch, and set it in the reeds by the Nile. She did not abandon him; she placed him in God's hands at the water's edge. Pharaoh's daughter found the child, and — by his sister's cleverness — Jochebed was paid to nurse her own son. She raised the deliverer of Israel in the very shadow of Pharaoh's palace.

Pharaoh's daughterExodus 2:5–10 ↗

She came to the Nile to bathe and saw the basket among the reeds. She opened it, saw the crying child, and had compassion on him: "This is one of the Hebrews' children." She named him Moses — "drawn out" — and raised him in the palace of the king who had commanded his death. A princess's compassion turned a death sentence into a rescue, and Moses grew up with both the palace and the people in him.

RahabJoshua 2 ↗

A prostitute in Jericho, in a house built into the city wall. When Joshua sent two spies into the land, she hid them on her roof and told the king's men they had gone. She had heard of Israel's God and believed before she ever met a Hebrew soldier: "The LORD your God, he is God in heaven above and on earth beneath" (Joshua 2:11). She secured a scarlet cord in her window as a sign, and when the walls fell, her household was spared. She was grafted into Israel, became the mother of Boaz, and her name appears in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). Faith found in a house of shame, and honoured forever.

Also appears in: Women of Exceptional Faith
Abigail1 Samuel 25 ↗

Wife of Nabal, a wealthy man described as harsh and badly behaved; Abigail is described as discerning and beautiful. When David — still an outlaw — sent his men to ask Nabal for provisions, Nabal insulted them, and David set out with 400 men to kill every male in the household. Abigail heard, took bread and wine and sheep, and went out to meet David on the road. She spoke with wisdom: "The LORD has restrained you from bloodguilt" (1 Samuel 25:26). David blessed her and turned back. After Nabal died, Abigail became David's wife. One well-timed, truthful word from a woman prevented a massacre.

Wise Woman of Abel2 Samuel 20 ↗

When Joab's army besieged the city of Abel Beth-maacah to capture the rebel Sheba, a wise woman stood on the wall and called out to Joab: "I am one of the peaceable and faithful in Israel." She negotiated with him, told the people what to do, and Sheba's head was thrown over the wall. The city was spared. Her name is not recorded; her wisdom is. Sometimes the one who saves a city is the one who speaks from the wall.

Also appears in: Women of Courage & Justice
Jehosheba2 Kings 11:2 ↗

A daughter of King Jehoram and sister of Ahaziah. When Athaliah the queen mother began killing the royal offspring to seize the throne, Jehosheba took Joash, Ahaziah's son, stole him from among the king's sons who were about to be killed, and hid him in the temple with his nurse for six years. Her act preserved the line of David, and Joash became king at age seven. A princess's courage in a single afternoon kept the promise of a king alive.

Also appears in: Women of Courage & Justice
Pilate's wifeMatthew 27:19 ↗

She is unnamed in Scripture — early tradition calls her Claudia Procula. While Pilate sat on the judgment seat, she sent him a message: "Have nothing to do with that righteous man, for I have suffered much because of him today in a dream." Pilate did not listen; he washed his hands and handed Jesus to the cross. Her warning stands in the record as a voice of conscience that a governor did not heed. Some women speak truth to power and are heard; some are not. Both are recorded.

4. Women of Exceptional Faith

SarahHebrews 11:11 ↗

Wife of Abraham, mother of Isaac. She waited decades for a son, then laughed when she heard the promise announced at ninety years old — laughed at the very idea of it. But God did not withdraw the promise; He kept it, and she bore Isaac. Hebrews 11:11 says she "received power to conceive" because "she considered him faithful who had promised." The New Testament honours her faith, not her laughter. She processed her doubt openly, and God grew her faith through it.

Hannah1 Samuel 1 ↗

Wife of Elkanah, and a woman deeply loved but deeply grieved. She was barren, and her husband's other wife, Peninnah, provoked her year after year. One year at Shiloh, Hannah slipped away from the family feast, wept, and prayed silently with such anguish that the priest Eli thought she was drunk. She poured out her soul before the LORD and vowed: if You give me a son, I will give him back to You. God answered — Samuel was born, and Hannah kept her vow. Her prayer in 1 Samuel 2 — "My heart exults in the LORD" — became a template for Mary's Magnificat centuries later (Luke 1:46–55). Persistent, honest prayer in grief is one of the most powerful things a woman can do.

Also appears in: Women Who Prayed
RuthRuth 1:16 ↗

A Moabite widow, daughter-in-law of the Israelite Naomi. When famine and death emptied the family, Naomi urged her two foreign daughters-in-law to return to Moab. Orpah went; Ruth clung to her and spoke the words that have been read at weddings and funerals for three thousand years: "Where you go I will go, and where you lodge I will lodge. Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16). She worked the fields of a man named Boaz, gleaning behind his reapers to feed her mother-in-law. Boaz redeemed her, and she became the great-grandmother of King David — and an ancestor of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). A foreigner bound herself to an old woman and a covenant she had no right to — and God grafted her into the lineage of the Messiah.

The Shunammite woman2 Kings 4:8 ↗

A wealthy woman of Shunem who recognised that the prophet Elisha was a holy man and urged him to stay for meals. She and her husband built a small room on the roof — a bed, a table, a chair, a lamp — and gave it to him. When Elisha asked what he could do for her, she said she had everything she needed. Then he learned she had no son, and he promised her one. When the boy was grown and died, she rode to the prophet and held his feet and would not let go until he came and raised him (2 Kings 4:27). Hospitality, faith, and holy stubbornness in one woman.

The Canaanite womanMatthew 15:28 ↗

A Gentile woman from the region of Tyre and Sidon, whose daughter was severely oppressed by a demon. She cried out to Jesus: "Have mercy on me, O Lord, Son of David." He answered her not a word; then He said He was sent only to the lost sheep of Israel. She came and knelt before Him. He told her it was not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs. She answered: "Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table." Jesus said to her: "O woman, great is your faith! Be it done for you as you desire." Her daughter was healed. She would not be turned away, and Jesus praised her persistence as faith.

The woman with the issue of bloodMark 5:34 ↗

She had suffered from a discharge of blood for twelve years. She had spent everything she had on physicians, and only grew worse. In a crowd pressing around Jesus on His way to Jairus' house, she came up behind Him and touched the fringe of His garment — because she thought, "If I touch even his garments, I will be made well." Immediately she felt in her body that she was healed. Jesus turned and asked who had touched Him, and she came forward in fear and trembling and told Him the whole truth. He said: "Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace" (Mark 5:34). After twelve years of being ceremonially unclean, she was called "Daughter" in front of everyone.

MaryLuke 1:38 ↗

A young woman in Nazareth, betrothed to a carpenter named Joseph, visited by an angel who told her she would bear a son by the Holy Spirit. Her response is one of the greatest statements of faith in Scripture: "Behold, I am the servant of the Lord; let it be to me according to your word" (Luke 1:38). She said yes before she understood. She sang a song that has been prayed in the church for two thousand years — "My soul magnifies the Lord" (Luke 1:46–55) — a song about God lifting the humble and bringing down the proud. She stood at the cross (John 19:25) and prayed with the disciples in the upper room (Acts 1:14). Her yes opened the door of the incarnation.

Lois & Eunice2 Timothy 1:5 ↗

Timothy's grandmother and mother. Paul writes to Timothy: "I am reminded of your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well." The names are brief but the implication is enormous: Timothy's faith did not come from a seminary; it came from a mother and a grandmother who taught him the Scriptures from childhood (2 Timothy 3:15). Two generations of quiet faithfulness produced a leader of the early church.

5. Women Who Served & Supported Ministry

The women who followed JesusLuke 8:1–3 ↗

Luke names three: Mary called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out; Joanna, the wife of Chuza, Herod's household manager; and Susanna. "And many others," he adds, "who provided for them out of their means." They travelled with Jesus and the Twelve, and they funded the ministry. When the male disciples scattered at the crucifixion, these women stayed — at the cross (Matthew 27:55) and at the tomb (Matthew 28:1). They are the quiet financial engine of the gospel's first years.

Martha & Mary of BethanyJohn 11–12 ↗

Sisters, with their brother Lazarus, in the village of Bethany. Martha is the one who welcomed Jesus into her home and served — and later, when Lazarus died, it was Martha who went out to meet Jesus and made the great confession: "I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God" (John 11:27). Mary is the one who sat at Jesus' feet (Luke 10:39), and later anointed them with costly perfume (John 12:3). One served at table, one sat at His feet; both loved the same Lord in the same house. When Jesus said, "Mary has chosen the good portion," He was not scolding Martha for service — He was correcting her anxiety.

LydiaActs 16:15 ↗

A businesswoman from Thyatira, a dealer in purple cloth — a trade that made her wealthy and independent. She was in Philippi on business, worshipping with a group of women by the river on the Sabbath, when Paul came and spoke. The Lord opened her heart to pay attention to what was said. She was baptised with her household, and she urged Paul and his companions: "If you have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come to my house and stay." She opened her home and her purse, and the first church on European soil began in her house (Acts 16:40). The gospel entered Europe through a woman's hospitality.

Also appears in: Women Who Hosted Churches
PhoebeRomans 16:1 ↗

Paul's first word in Romans 16 is a commendation: "I commend to you our sister Phoebe, a servant of the church at Cenchreae." The Greek word is diakonos — the same word used for the office of deacon. She was likely the woman who carried Paul's letter to the Romans — the most important theological letter in history — from Corinth to Rome. Paul tells the Roman church to "help her in whatever she may need," because she has been a patron of many, including Paul himself. A woman entrusted with the gospel's most weighty letter, and honoured by name.

Dorcas (Tabitha)Acts 9:36 ↗

A disciple in Joppa, "full of good works and acts of charity." She made garments for widows — the poor, the vulnerable, the forgotten. When she became ill and died, the disciples in Joppa sent for Peter. When he arrived, the widows stood around him weeping and showing the tunics and garments Dorcas had made while she was with them. Peter prayed, and she opened her eyes (Acts 9:40). Her story is not remembered for great speeches or visions; it is remembered for clothing she sewed for widows. That is what God raised her for.

The widow with two mitesMark 12:41–44 ↗

Jesus sat opposite the temple treasury and watched people put money into the offering box. Many rich people put in large sums. A poor widow came and put in two small copper coins — worth a fraction of a day's wage. Jesus called His disciples and said: "Truly, I say to you, this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the offering box. For they all contributed out of their abundance, but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on." She gave out of her poverty, and Jesus made her the standard of generosity for all time.

6. Women Who Taught & Discipled

PriscillaActs 18:26 ↗

Wife of Aquila, a tentmaker like Paul, and Paul's co-worker in the gospel. When they met a gifted preacher named Apollos in Ephesus, and heard him teach accurately but incompletely, "Priscilla and Aquila took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately" (Acts 18:26). She is named first in several passages — including in Romans 16:3, where Paul calls her and Aquila "my fellow workers in Christ Jesus." She hosted a church in her house (Romans 16:5). A married woman who taught a preacher, hosted a church, and is honoured by name in Scripture.

Also appears in: Women Who Served & Supported Ministry · Women Who Hosted Churches
Older womenTitus 2:3–5 ↗

Paul's instruction to Titus is not an afterthought; it is a structural role in the church's discipleship. "Older women likewise are to be reverent in behaviour, not slanderers or slaves to much wine. They are to teach what is good, and so train the young women to love their husbands and children, to be self-controlled, pure, working at home, kind, and submissive to their own husbands, that the word of God may not be reviled." Paul assigns older women a teaching ministry — not to the congregation, but to younger women. It is a real calling, and the health of the church depends on it.

The Proverbs 31 womanProverbs 31:10–31 ↗

A poem — an acrostic in Hebrew, each verse beginning with a successive letter of the alphabet. She is not a single historical woman; she is a portrait of wisdom embodied. She rises before dawn, provides for her household, buys a field, plants a vineyard, trades profitably, cares for the poor, speaks with wisdom, and her children call her blessed. Her husband trusts her and praises her. "Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised" (Proverbs 31:30). The poem is not a checklist for women to fail; it is a portrait of a woman who fears God, and a promise that such a woman will be honoured.

7. Women Who Evangelized

The Samaritan womanJohn 4:29 ↗

A Samaritan woman — doubly marginalised, by her ethnicity and by her five failed marriages. Jesus met her at a well at midday, alone. He asked her for water, and then told her about living water, and then told her everything she had ever done. She left her water jar at the well and went into the town and said: "Come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Can this be the Christ?" Many Samaritans believed because of her testimony (John 4:39). She was the first evangelist in John's Gospel, and the first person in that Gospel to call Jesus the Christ. A woman with a broken past became the conduit for a whole town to meet its Saviour.

Mary MagdaleneJohn 20:18 ↗

Mary from the town of Magdala, from whom Jesus had cast out seven demons (Luke 8:2). She followed Him from Galilee, supported His ministry, and stood at the cross when the male disciples had fled (John 19:25). On the first day of the week she came to the tomb while it was still dark, found it empty, and ran to tell Peter and John. When she returned and wept at the tomb, Jesus appeared to her and called her by name (John 20:16). She went to the disciples and announced: "I have seen the Lord" (John 20:18). She is the first witness of the resurrection, and the church has called her "the apostle to the apostles."

The women at the tombMatthew 28:1–10 ↗

Mary Magdalene and the other Mary came to see the tomb at dawn on the first day of the week. There was a great earthquake, and an angel descended, rolled back the stone, and sat on it. He told them: "Do not be afraid, for I know that you seek Jesus who was crucified. He is not here, for he has risen, as he said. Come, see the place where he lay. Then go quickly and tell his disciples…" They departed quickly with fear and great joy and ran to tell His disciples. And Jesus met them on the way. They came to anoint a body and left with a commission — the first preachers of the resurrection.

8. Women Who Prayed

The women in the upper roomActs 1:14 ↗

After the ascension, the disciples returned to Jerusalem and went to the upper room, where they devoted themselves to prayer — "with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers." When the day of Pentecost came, they were all together in one place, and the Spirit fell (Acts 2:1–4). The church was born in a prayer meeting, and women were in it. They did not spectate; they prayed.

Euodia & SyntychePhilippians 4:2–3 ↗

Two women in the church at Philippi, named by Paul in a letter read aloud to the whole congregation. They had "laboured side by side with me in the gospel," Paul writes — the same verb he uses for his own apostolic work. But they were in conflict, and Paul urges them to agree in the Lord, and asks a "true companion" to help them. The church's earliest recorded internal dispute was between two women who had laboured in the gospel — and it was serious enough for Paul to address it publicly, and hopeful enough that he did not give up on them.

9. Women Who Hosted Churches

NymphaColossians 4:15 ↗

Paul closes his letter to the Colossians with greetings, including: "Give my greetings to the brothers at Laodicea, and to Nympha and the church in her house." A woman whose home was a congregation — the gathering point for believers in Laodicea or nearby. Her name in the letter is the only record we have of her, and it is enough. Some churches are pastored; some are hosted. Nympha's was hosted.

ApphiaPhilemon 2 ↗

Paul addresses his letter to Philemon to "our beloved fellow worker Philemon, and Apphia our sister, and Archippus our fellow soldier, and the church in your house." Apphia is named third, alongside her husband (likely) and a fellow worker. She was probably the hostess of the church that gathered in the house, and the household matriarch whose consent mattered for Paul's appeal to receive Onesimus back as a brother. A woman whose hospitality and authority made the letter possible.

10. Women of Courage & Justice

VashtiEsther 1:12 ↗

Queen to King Ahasuerus (likely Xerxes I) of Persia. At a great feast, on the seventh day, when the king was drunk, he commanded his eunuchs to bring Queen Vashti before him wearing her royal crown, to display her beauty to the peoples and officials. She refused. The king was furious, and his advisers — worried that other women would hear of it and despise their husbands — advised him to depose her and replace her with a more compliant queen. He did. She lost her crown. She kept her dignity. And the decree that replaced her prepared the way for Esther's "yes." One woman's "no" — even when it cost her everything — prepared the way for another woman's "yes."

Rizpah2 Samuel 21:10 ↗

A concubine of King Saul, mother of two of his sons. When the Gibeonites demanded seven of Saul's sons be handed over for execution to atone for Saul's earlier crimes, David gave them Rizpah's two sons, along with five others. They were executed and their bodies left exposed. Rizpah took sackcloth and spread it on a rock, and from the beginning of harvest until rain fell on the bodies, she did not let the birds of the air rest on them by day or the beasts of the field by night. Months of vigil. When David heard what she had done, he gathered the bones and gave the dead a proper burial (2 Samuel 21:14). A mother's persistent love moved a king.

Tamar (Judah's daughter-in-law)Genesis 38:26 ↗

Wife of Er, Judah's firstborn, then of Onan after Er died, then promised to Judah's third son Shelah when he grew up. Judah did not keep his promise, and Tamar was left childless in a culture where that meant destitution. When Judah's wife died, Tamar disguised herself as a prostitute, sat at the entrance to Enaim, and Judah — not knowing who she was — lay with her. She took his signet, cord, and staff as a pledge. When she was found pregnant, Judah ordered her burned. She produced the pledge, and Judah said: "She is more righteous than I, since I did not give her to my son Shelah" (Genesis 38:26). She risked everything for justice and for a place in the family of promise, and her son Perez became an ancestor of King David — and of Jesus (Matthew 1:3). Her story is uncomfortable, and God wrote it into the line of the Messiah.

Wise Woman of Tekoa2 Samuel 14 ↗

When Absalom fled after killing his brother Amnon, David mourned for him but would not bring him home. Joab, David's general, sent a wise woman from Tekoa to David with a story — a fictional account of her own "loss," designed to draw David out. She came in disguise, told her tale, drew David into a pronouncement, and then revealed the truth: David had just decreed his own situation. David saw through the story and asked if Joab was behind it. She answered honestly. David brought Absalom home. A woman's story and a woman's wisdom turned a king's heart and reversed a broken family situation.
