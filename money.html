A Note Before You Begin

This page is a biblical study on money, work, and contentment—not a prosperity gospel and not a poverty gospel. It surveys what Scripture actually says about God's ownership of everything, our role as stewards, the danger and usefulness of money, the dignity of work, and the freedom of contentment. The aim is not to make you feel guilty about what you have or hopeless about what you lack, but to set you free to use money as a tool for the kingdom. Read the verses, and let them search your heart.

Money, Work & Contentment — A Biblical Study

1. God's Ownership & Our Stewardship

The Earth Is the Lord'sPsalm 24:1-2 ↗

Everything begins with ownership, and Scripture is clear about who owns what. The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof, the world and those who dwell therein (Psalm 24:1). The cattle on a thousand hills are His (Psalm 50:10), the silver and the gold are His (Haggai 2:8), and every beast of the forest is His (Psalm 50:10). We do not own anything in an absolute sense; we hold it in trust. This single truth reframes everything about money. If God owns it all, then money is not ultimately mine to hoard, to flaunt, or to obsess over—it is His to direct. The question changes from How much can I keep? to How does the Owner want me to use what He has entrusted to me? The believer who really believes Psalm 24:1 sleeps differently, gives differently, and worries differently than the believer who functionally lives as if they are the owner.

Application: Remind yourself this week: everything you have belongs to God—you are a steward, not an owner.
Supporting references: Psalm 50:10-12; 1 Corinthians 10:26; Haggai 2:8
We Are Stewards, Not Owners1 Corinthians 4:1-2 ↗

Paul describes himself and every Christian leader as a steward of the mysteries of God (1 Corinthians 4:1). A steward in the ancient world was not the owner of the household; he was the trusted manager, answerable to the master for what had been entrusted to him. God gave humanity dominion over the earth in Genesis 1:28, not absolute ownership but delegated authority—the right to cultivate, care for, and use creation as God's representative. Jesus' parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30) makes the same point: the master entrusts his property to servants, and they will give an account. Every dollar, every skill, every opportunity, every relationship is a trust. The steward's job is not to ask What is mine? but What does the Master want done with what He has placed in my hands? Paul adds the one thing required of stewards: that they be found faithful (1 Corinthians 4:2). Faithfulness, not accumulation, is the measure.

Application: Ask God this week: "What do You want me to do with what You have entrusted to me?"—and then do it.
Supporting references: Genesis 1:28; Psalm 8:6-8; Matthew 25:14-30
The Parable of the TalentsMatthew 25:14-30 ↗

Jesus tells the story of a master who entrusts his property to three servants—five talents to one, two to another, one to the third, each according to his ability. The first two servants trade and double what they were given; the third buries his talent in the ground and returns it exactly as received. The master's verdict on the third servant is one of the most sobering sentences in Scripture: You wicked and slothful servant! You knew that I reap where I have not sown and gather where I scattered no seed? Then you ought to have invested my money with the bankers, and at my coming I should have received what was my own with interest (Matthew 25:26-27). The third servant was condemned not for losing money but for doing nothing with it. The parable is not mainly about investment strategy; it is about stewardship—about the reality that God has entrusted each of us with time, talent, and treasure, and He expects us to put them to work for the kingdom. The question is not whether you have more or less than others. The question is whether you are faithful with what you have.

Application: Identify one gift or resource you have been burying—and put it to work for God this week.
Supporting references: Luke 19:11-27; Romans 14:12; 2 Corinthians 5:10
We Will Give an AccountRomans 14:10-12 ↗

Paul writes, For we will all stand before the judgment seat of God; for it is written, As I live, says the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God. So then each of us will give an account of himself to God (Romans 14:10-12). This is not a doctrine of condemnation—there is no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1)—but it is a doctrine of accountability. The believer will stand before Christ and give an account of how they used what He entrusted to them: the money, the time, the opportunities, the relationships, the spiritual gifts. Hebrews says, no creature is hidden from his sight, but all are naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account (Hebrews 4:13). This is not meant to make you afraid; it is meant to make you serious. The way you spend your money is not a private matter; it is a matter for which you will one day answer to the One who gave it to you.

Application: Live today knowing you will give an account to God for how you use what He has given you—and let that make you faithful.
Supporting references: 2 Corinthians 5:10; Hebrews 4:13; Ecclesiastes 12:14

2. Money: A Tool, Not a Master

You Cannot Serve God and MoneyMatthew 6:24 ↗

Jesus speaks one of the most direct sentences in the Sermon on the Mount: No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and money (Matthew 6:24). The Greek word for serve is douleuo, meaning to be a slave to. Jesus is not saying you cannot have money; He is saying you cannot be a slave to both God and money. One will always win. Money is a good servant but a terrible master. Paul tells Timothy, the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils (1 Timothy 6:10). Note carefully: it is the love of money, not money itself, that is the root. Money is morally neutral; it is our relationship to it that is dangerous. Hebrews says, Keep your life free from love of money, and be content with what you have, for he has said, I will never leave you nor forsake you (Hebrews 13:5). The question is never Do I have too much? but Who is my master?

Application: Ask yourself honestly: is money a tool in your hand, or a master over your heart?
Supporting references: Luke 16:13; 1 Timothy 6:10; Hebrews 13:5
Where Your Treasure IsMatthew 6:19-21 ↗

Jesus gives one of the clearest tests of the heart: Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal, but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust destroys and where thieves do not break in and steal. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also (Matthew 6:19-21). Notice the order: your treasure goes first, and your heart follows. We often think the opposite—we think we will put our money where our heart is. Jesus says the heart follows the money. So if you want to know what your heart really values, look at your bank statement, not your intentions. Jesus tells the parable of the rich fool (Luke 12:15-21) to make the point: a man with full barns dies that very night, and the question is asked, the things you have prepared, whose will they be? Every dollar we hold is a temporary trust; what we do with it, however, has eternal consequences.

Application: Look at your last month of spending—does it show where your heart really is? Let it convict you, then adjust.
Supporting references: Luke 12:15-21; Colossians 3:1-2; 1 John 2:15-17
The Rich FoolLuke 12:13-21 ↗

A man in the crowd asked Jesus to tell his brother to divide the family inheritance with him. Jesus answered with a warning: Take care, and be on your guard against all covetousness, for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions (Luke 12:15). Then He told the parable: a rich man's land produced a great harvest, and he said to himself, What shall I do, for I have nowhere to store my crops? And he said, I will do this: I will tear down my barns and build larger ones, and there I will store all my grain and my goods. And I will say to my soul, Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry. But God said to him, Fool! This night your soul is required of you, and the things you have prepared, whose will they be? (Luke 12:17-20). The rich fool is not condemned for his business acumen; he is condemned for his forgetfulness of God. He built bigger barns for a longer life that he was never going to have. The parable is a mirror for every believer tempted to think security comes from the bank account rather than from the Giver.

Application: Ask yourself today: what barn am I building, and would God call it foolish?
Supporting references: Psalm 49:10-12; Proverbs 27:1; Ecclesiastes 5:10-15
Do Not Trust in Uncertain Riches1 Timothy 6:17-19 ↗

Paul writes to Timothy with a word for those who are rich in this present age: As for the rich in this present age, charge them not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy. They are to do good, to be rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share, thus storing up treasure for themselves as a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of that which is truly life (1 Timothy 6:17-19). Notice the two dangers Paul names: pride and misplaced hope. Riches are uncertain—they fluctuate, they fail, they vanish. God is certain—He richly provides for us to enjoy. The prescription is not poverty but generosity: the rich are to be rich in good works, generous, ready to share. David wrote, if riches increase, set not your heart on them (Psalm 62:10). Solomon wrote, Whoever trusts in his riches will fall, but the righteous will flourish like a green leaf (Proverbs 11:28). The rich are not forbidden from having money; they are commanded to hold it loosely and use it generously.

Application: If God has given you more than you need, ask Him how He wants you to use the excess for His kingdom.
Supporting references: Psalm 62:10; Proverbs 11:28; Mark 10:24-25
The Deceitfulness of RichesMark 4:18-19 ↗

Jesus explains the parable of the sower, and one of the seeds falls among thorns. He says, And others are the ones sown among thorns. They are those who hear the word, but the cares of the world and the deceitfulness of riches and the desires for other things enter in and choke the word, and it proves unfruitful (Mark 4:18-19). Notice the phrase deceitfulness of riches. Money promises what it cannot deliver: security, peace, happiness, freedom, identity. It is deceptive precisely because it delivers some of what it promises—it does give some comfort, some options, some status—but never the peace or the identity that only God gives. Paul warns, those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare, into many senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction (1 Timothy 6:9). Proverbs observes that the rich man's wealth is his strong city, imagining it is an impregnable wall (Proverbs 18:11)—but no wall saves from death, and no wall saves from God's judgment. Riches are not evil; they are just deceitful, and we are easily deceived.

Application: Identify one lie money has whispered to you—and answer it with a promise from God.
Supporting references: 1 Timothy 6:9-10; Matthew 13:22; Proverbs 18:11
Do Not Worry About MoneyMatthew 6:25-34 ↗

Jesus spends eleven verses in the Sermon on the Mount on this single command: do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink, nor about your body, what you will put on (Matthew 6:25). He points to the birds of the air, who neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet our heavenly Father feeds them (Matthew 6:26). He points to the lilies of the field, which are arrayed more beautifully than Solomon in all his glory (Matthew 6:28-29). Then He asks the deeper question: But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you (Matthew 6:33). The antidote to financial anxiety is not more money; it is a reordered priority. When God is first, money finds its place. When money is first, anxiety takes over. Peter writes, Cast all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you (1 Peter 5:7). The believer who trusts the Father does not have to be eaten by worry.

Application: Take your biggest financial fear to God today—name it, pray it, and trust the Father who clothes the lilies.
Supporting references: Philippians 4:6-7; 1 Peter 5:7; Hebrews 13:5
Godliness with Contentment1 Timothy 6:6-8 ↗

Paul writes to Timothy: But godliness with contentment is great gain, for we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world. But if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content (1 Timothy 6:6-8). The world says the opposite: gain is measured by what you accumulate. Paul says the greatest gain is godliness with contentment—a life that fears God and is satisfied with what He provides. Notice the reality check: we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out. Every cent we have is temporary, and every cent will be left behind. If you have food and clothing, Paul says, that is enough. This is not a call to poverty or to neglect; it is a call to see that contentment is a spiritual discipline, not a financial condition. You can be anxious in a mansion and content in a hut. The issue is the heart, and the discipline of contentment is learned, not inherited—which is why Paul says, I have learned to be content (Philippians 4:11).

Application: Practice this prayer this week: "Lord, You are enough—teach me to be content with what You give me."
Supporting references: Hebrews 13:5; Philippians 4:11-13; Proverbs 15:16

3. Work as Worship

Work Was God's IdeaGenesis 2:15 ↗

Before the fall, before sin entered the world, God placed the man in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it (Genesis 2:15). Work is not a curse; it is a creation ordinance. God gave humanity a job before He gave them a warning. In Genesis 1:28, the first command to humanity is a work command: be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it, and have dominion over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the heavens and over every living thing that moves on the earth. Work is part of what it means to be made in God's image—God Himself works (John 5:17), and He made us to work alongside Him. Ecclesiastes says, everyone should eat and drink and take pleasure in all his toil—this is God's gift to man (Ecclesiastes 3:13). Work is not something to escape; it is a gift to be received, a calling to be fulfilled, a place where God meets us. The Christian does not work to escape the world; he works to serve it in Christ's name.

Application: Reframe your work today—see it not as a curse to endure but a calling to fulfill before God.
Supporting references: Genesis 1:28; Psalm 8:6-8; Ecclesiastes 3:13
Work as unto the LordColossians 3:23-24 ↗

Paul writes, Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men, knowing that from the Lord you will receive the inheritance as your reward. You are serving the Lord Christ (Colossians 3:23-24). This single verse changes everything about how a Christian works. The audience is no longer the boss, the customer, the co-worker, or the critic—the audience is Christ. The work is no longer ultimately for the paycheck; it is for the Lord who sees in secret and rewards openly. This does not make the work less important; it makes it infinitely more important. A Christian sweeping floors is doing work as sacred as a pastor preaching a sermon, if both are done as unto the Lord. Paul tells the Corinthians, So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God (1 Corinthians 10:31). And he tells the Romans, Do not be slothful in zeal, be fervent in spirit, serve the Lord (Romans 12:11). The Christian who works as unto the Lord cannot be lazy, cannot be sloppy, cannot cut corners, because Christ is watching.

Application: Before you work today, ask: "Jesus, I am doing this for You—what would You have me do?"
Supporting references: Ephesians 6:5-8; 1 Corinthians 10:31; Romans 12:11
The Sluggard and the AntProverbs 6:6-11 ↗

Proverbs gives us a vivid picture of the sluggard—the lazy person. Go to the ant, O sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise. Without having any chief, officer, or ruler, she prepares her bread in summer and gathers her food in harvest (Proverbs 6:6-8). The ant has no supervisor, no deadline, no boss standing over her—and yet she works with industry and foresight. The sluggard has every excuse: The sluggard says, There is a lion in the road! There is a lion in the streets! (Proverbs 26:13)—there is always a reason to not work. And the warning is severe: A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest, and poverty will come upon you like a robber, and want like an armed man (Proverbs 6:10-11). The Bible is not against rest—it commands rest, it commands the Sabbath—but it is against the sluggard's self-destructive laziness. Work is part of how God provides for us, and the believer is called to labor honestly, industriously, and with foresight.

Application: Deal with the one area of laziness in your life this week—do the work you've been avoiding.
Supporting references: Proverbs 10:4; Proverbs 13:4; Proverbs 26:13-16
If Anyone Is Not Willing to Work2 Thessalonians 3:6-12 ↗

Paul writes to the Thessalonians, For even when we were with you, we would give you this command: If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat (2 Thessalonians 3:10). This is a strong word, but it is a biblical one. Paul is not talking about those unable to work—the sick, the elderly, the disabled—but about those who can work and refuse. He had already told the Thessalonians, to aspire to live quietly, to mind your own affairs, and to work with your hands (1 Thessalonians 4:11). He practiced what he preached, working as a tentmaker so he would not be a burden (2 Thessalonians 3:8). And he tells the Ephesians, Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need (Ephesians 4:28). Notice the goal of work here: not just self-provision, but generosity. Work is a gift that gives you something to give away.

Application: Commit to honest labor this week—and ask how your work can become generosity to someone in need.
Supporting references: 1 Thessalonians 4:11-12; Proverbs 6:9-11; Ephesians 4:28
Rest Is Also HolyGenesis 2:2-3 ↗

Work is holy, and so is rest. The very first thing God does after creating the world is to rest. And on the seventh day God finished his work that he had done, and he rested on the seventh day from all his work that he had done. So God blessed the seventh day and made it holy (Genesis 2:2-3). The rest of God is not a sign of weakness; it is a declaration that the world does not depend on us. God commanded Israel to rest on the Sabbath (Exodus 20:8-11), and Jesus said, The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath (Mark 2:27)—rest is a gift, not a burden. Hebrews says, So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God (Hebrews 4:9). Christians are called to live a rhythm of work and rest that mirrors God's own rhythm. That means: you are not the world's Savior, you cannot work all the time without breaking, and you are commanded to stop. The believer who cannot rest is a believer who has forgotten that God is on the throne—and that He does not need their twenty-four-hour attention to keep the world running.

Application: Take one full day of rest this week—no work, no screens, just God and the people you love.
Supporting references: Exodus 20:8-11; Mark 2:27; Hebrews 4:9-10
Do Not Be Anxious About Tomorrow's WorkMatthew 6:34 ↗

Jesus says, Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble (Matthew 6:34). The Christian who works as unto the Lord still lives one day at a time. Jesus is not discouraging foresight; He is discouraging anxiety about tomorrow. Proverbs says, Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring (Proverbs 27:1). James says, Come now, you who say, Today or tomorrow we will go into such and such a town and spend a year there and trade and make a profit—yet you do not know what tomorrow will bring. What is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes. Instead you ought to say, If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that (James 4:13-15). The Psalmist adds, It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep (Psalm 127:2). Work faithfully today, and leave tomorrow in God's hands. He will provide, as He always has.

Application: Do today's work faithfully, and hand tomorrow's work to God—He is already there.
Supporting references: Proverbs 27:1; James 4:13-15; Psalm 127:2

4. Generosity & Contentment

God Loves a Cheerful Giver2 Corinthians 9:6-8 ↗

Paul writes, The point is this: whoever sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and whoever sows bountifully will also reap bountifully. Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver (2 Corinthians 9:6-7). Notice that Paul is not commanding a specific amount; he is commanding a specific attitude. Give as you have decided in your heart—not under compulsion, not out of guilt, but cheerfully. And then the great promise: And God is able to make all grace abound to you, so that having all sufficiency in all things at all times, you may abound in every good work (2 Corinthians 9:8). Generosity is not a drain on your resources; it is a channel of God's grace. Luke records Jesus saying, Give, and it will be given to you. Good measure, pressed down, shaken together, running over, will be put into your lap (Luke 6:38). And Paul quotes Jesus (who did not say it in the Gospels): It is more blessed to give than to receive (Acts 20:35). The cheerful giver is not someone who has more than enough; it is someone who trusts God to provide.

Application: Give something away this week—not because you can afford it, but because you trust God to provide.
Supporting references: Proverbs 11:24-25; Luke 6:38; Acts 20:35
The Widow's Two MitesMark 12:41-44 ↗

Jesus sat opposite the temple treasury and watched people put money into the offering box. Many rich people put in large sums. And a poor widow came and put in two small copper coins, which make a penny (Mark 12:41-42). Jesus called His disciples and said, Truly, I say to you, this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the offering box. For they all contributed out of their abundance, but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on (Mark 12:43-44). The widow's gift is the clearest picture in Scripture that generosity is measured by the heart, not by the amount. She gave two copper coins; the rich gave much more. But she gave more than all of them, because she gave out of her poverty, and she gave all she had. The question generosity asks is not How much did you give? but How much of yourself did you give? and How much did you trust God when you gave?

Application: Do not measure your generosity by the amount—measure it by the trust it shows in God.
Supporting references: Luke 21:1-4; 2 Corinthians 8:1-5; Philippians 4:18
The Macedonian Generosity2 Corinthians 8:1-5 ↗

Paul tells the Corinthians about the churches of Macedonia, who, in the midst of extreme poverty, gave with overflowing joy. We want you to know, brothers, about the grace of God that has been given among the churches of Macedonia, for in a severe test of affliction, their abundance of joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity on their part. For they gave according to their means, as I can testify, and beyond their means, of their own accord, begging us earnestly for the favor of taking part in the relief of the saints (2 Corinthians 8:1-4). This is one of the most remarkable passages in Scripture. A church in poverty, under affliction, begged to give more. They did not give because they were commanded; they gave because they had received grace, and grace overflowed in generosity. And their giving was not just money—they first gave themselves to the Lord and to Paul by the will of God (2 Corinthians 8:5). True generosity begins with giving yourself to God, and everything else follows.

Application: Ask God for the grace of generosity—a grace that overflows even when your circumstances are tight.
Supporting references: Philippians 4:15-19; Romans 15:25-27; Acts 2:44-45
Contentment Is LearnedPhilippians 4:11-13 ↗

Paul writes, Not that I am speaking of being in need, for I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content. I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need. I can do all things through him who strengthens me (Philippians 4:11-13). Notice the word learned. Contentment is not a personality trait; it is a learned discipline. Paul learned it in prison, in hunger, in abundance, in need. And notice the famous verse in its true context: I can do all things through him who strengthens me is not a promise of athletic victory or business success; it is a promise of contentment in Christ in every circumstance. The Christian who has learned contentment is free from the tyranny of comparison, the slavery of consumerism, and the anxiety of always needing more. They have what they need, and they have Christ—and Christ is enough.

Application: Ask God to teach you the secret Paul learned—contentment in Christ, in every circumstance.
Supporting references: Hebrews 13:5; 1 Timothy 6:6-8; Psalm 23:1
The Lord Is My ShepherdPsalm 23:1-6 ↗

The most beloved psalm in Scripture begins with six words that answer every financial fear: The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want (Psalm 23:1). David does not say The LORD is my boss or The LORD is my employer, though both are true. He says The LORD is my shepherd. A shepherd provides for the sheep, protects the sheep, guides the sheep, and knows each sheep by name. If the Lord is your shepherd, then your deepest need is already met—not because you have everything you want, but because you have everything you need. Jesus says, I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). Ezekiel promised that God Himself would come and shepherd His people (Ezekiel 34:11-16)—and Jesus is the fulfillment of that promise. Revelation says, the Lamb in the midst of the throne will be their shepherd, and he will guide them to springs of living water, and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes (Revelation 7:17). The believer who truly knows the Shepherd does not have to be consumed by financial anxiety, because the Shepherd provides.

Application: Pray Psalm 23 slowly this week, and let each line re-shape your trust.
Supporting references: John 10:11-14; Ezekiel 34:11-16; Revelation 7:17
Do Not Love the World's Money1 John 2:15-17 ↗

John writes, Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him. For all that is in the world—the desires of the flesh and the desires of the eyes and pride of life—is not from the Father but is from the world. And the world is passing away along with its desires, but whoever does the will of God abides forever (1 John 2:15-17). John is not against enjoying creation or being grateful for what God provides; he is against loving the world—being shaped by its desires, its values, its ambitions. James says, You adulterous people! Do you not know that friendship with the world is enmity with God? (James 4:4). The love of the world and the love of the Father are mutually exclusive; one crowds out the other. The world is passing away—every dollar in your bank account, every status symbol, every trophy, every possession. What abides forever is the will of God. The Christian who has learned to love the Father and His will more than the world is free of the tyranny of materialism.

Application: Ask yourself honestly: what part of the world have I been loving? Take it to God and ask Him to free you.
Supporting references: James 4:4; Matthew 6:24; Colossians 3:5
Give to God What Is God'sMatthew 22:15-22 ↗

The Pharisees tried to trap Jesus with a question about paying taxes to Caesar. They thought they had cornered Him into either offending Rome or offending the people. Jesus asked for a coin, and said, Whose likeness and inscription is this? They said, Caesar's. Then he said to them, Therefore render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's (Matthew 22:19-21). The line is not between secular and sacred; it is between what is Caesar's and what is God's. Caesar's coin belongs to Caesar; the coin bears Caesar's image. But the human being belongs to God; the human being bears God's image (Genesis 1:27). The command is not Do not pay taxes or Do not be involved in the world; the command is Be sure you give to God what belongs to God. Everything belongs to God—your life, your money, your time, your skills, your relationships. The Christian who renders to Caesar what is Caesar's and to God what is God's has the right order: God first, everything else second.

Application: Look at your life today—are you giving God what is God's, or only what is left over?
Supporting references: Romans 13:1-7; Psalm 24:1; 1 Peter 2:13-17
Seek First the KingdomMatthew 6:33 ↗

The single sentence that reorders every financial decision in your life: But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you (Matthew 6:33). What things? The food, the drink, the clothing—the basic provisions of life that Jesus has just told us not to be anxious about. The promise is not that you will be rich; the promise is that you will be provided for. The condition is not a formula—it is a priority. Seek first the kingdom of God. Everything else falls in line behind that. Jesus tells the parable of the hidden treasure (Matthew 13:44) and the pearl of great value (Matthew 13:45-46) to show what the kingdom is worth: a man sells everything he has to buy the field, and a merchant sells everything he has to buy the pearl. The kingdom is worth more than everything else combined. The Christian who has truly grasped that is free from the anxiety of money, the slavery of money, and the tyranny of comparison. He seeks first the kingdom, and everything else is added.
