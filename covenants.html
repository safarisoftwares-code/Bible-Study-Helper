A Note Before You Begin

This page surveys the covenants Scripture names, from the cosmic covenant with Noah to the new covenant in Christ's blood. Some are covenants of redemptive history—Noahic, Abrahamic, Mosaic, Davidic, New—and carry the weight of the whole Bible's storyline. Others are covenants between individuals or within Israel's life—Rahab, Jonathan and David, marriage—and carry their own smaller but real significance. Two entries, Jacob at Bethel and Rahab at Jericho, are promises or oaths where Scripture does not use the word covenant at the moment of the event; they are included and marked because many study Bibles treat them as covenant renewals or covenant-adjacent, and they belong to the same fabric. Read prayerfully, and let these notes be a doorway rather than a destination.

The Covenants of Scripture — In Order

The Noahic CovenantGenesis 9:8-17 ↗

The world had become so violent that God looked upon it and grieved (Genesis 6:6). Into that ruin came a command to build an ark, and with it a promise that God had not abandoned His creation (Genesis 6:18). After the floodwaters receded and Noah stood on dry ground, God established a covenant not only with Noah but with every living creature (Genesis 9:12-13). The sign was a bow in the clouds—a rainbow—set in the sky as a reminder that God would never again destroy the earth by flood (Genesis 9:13-15). This first covenant is striking in its generosity: it binds God to a promise He will keep regardless of human faithfulness (Genesis 8:21-22). The rainbow is not a symbol of human goodness but of divine restraint. When you see one, you are looking at a pledge made before you were born.

Application: When you see a rainbow, pause and thank God for His covenant faithfulness that does not depend on your performance.
Supporting references: Genesis 6:18; Genesis 8:21-22; Genesis 9:12-13
The Abrahamic CovenantGenesis 15:1-21 ↗

God spoke to a man in a pagan city and told him to leave everything familiar for a land he had never seen, promising land, offspring, and blessing for all peoples on earth (Genesis 12:1-3). Abraham waited years without a son, until God took him outside and told him to count the stars (Genesis 15:5). When Abraham believed, it was credited to him as righteousness (Genesis 15:6), a verse Paul later built an entire gospel upon (Romans 4:3). The covenant was sealed with a solemn ceremony where God alone passed between the divided animals, binding Himself to the promise while Abraham slept (Genesis 15:17). Later God added the sign of circumcision (Genesis 17:10-11) and confirmed the covenant after the near-sacrifice of Isaac on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22:15-18). This covenant is the root from which the whole tree of redemption grows.

Application: Trust that God's promises rest on His faithfulness, not on your ability to earn them.
Supporting references: Genesis 12:1-3; Genesis 17:1-14; Genesis 22:15-18; Romans 4:3
The Covenant with IsaacGenesis 17:19-21 ↗

When Abraham pleaded for Ishmael, God answered clearly: no, Sarah your wife shall bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac, and I will establish my covenant with him as an everlasting covenant for his offspring after him (Genesis 17:19). The covenant was not passed to the son of the flesh but to the son of promise. God reaffirmed it to Isaac at Gerar during a famine: sojourn in this land, and I will be with you and will bless you, for to you and to your offspring I will give all these lands, and I will establish the oath that I swore to Abraham your father (Genesis 26:3). Paul later builds on this: not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel, and not all are children of Abraham because they are his offspring, but through Isaac shall your offspring be named (Romans 9:6-7). The covenant with Isaac is the covenant with Abraham, repeated—and the repetition itself teaches that God's promise runs by election and grace, not by natural descent.

Application: Rest in the fact that God's covenant runs by His promise, not by your lineage or your effort.
Supporting references: Genesis 26:3-5; Romans 9:7-9
Jacob's Covenant at BethelGenesis 28:10-22 ↗

Jacob, fleeing from his brother Esau, stopped for the night at Bethel and dreamed of a ladder reaching to heaven with angels ascending and descending on it (Genesis 28:12). The LORD stood above it and said: I am the LORD, the God of Abraham your father and the God of Isaac; the land on which you lie I will give to you and to your offspring, and in you and your offspring shall all the families of the earth be blessed, and behold, I am with you and will keep you wherever you go (Genesis 28:13-15). Jacob woke, set up a stone as a pillar, poured oil on it, and named the place Bethel—house of God (Genesis 28:18-19). Years later, God appeared to him again at Bethel and renewed the promise: I am God Almighty; be fruitful and multiply; a nation and a company of nations shall come from you, and kings shall come from your own body (Genesis 35:11). Scripture does not use the word covenant at the exact moment of the ladder dream, but the promise is the Abrahamic covenant passed on, and Genesis itself calls it a covenant in the surrounding context (Genesis 17:19, 21); many study Bibles treat Bethel as a covenant renewal. Its significance is that the covenant survives the failures of the recipient—Jacob the deceiver becomes Israel the father of the twelve tribes through whom the Messiah comes.

Application: God's covenant does not depend on your perfect record—He renews His promise to the undeserving.
Supporting references: Genesis 35:9-15; Genesis 46:1-4
The Mosaic CovenantExodus 19:3-8 ↗

Three months after Israel walked out of Egypt, they camped at a mountain that shook with thunder and smoke (Exodus 19:16-18). God offered Israel a covenant: if they obeyed His voice, they would be His treasured possession among all nations (Exodus 19:5-6). The people answered in unison that everything the LORD had said they would do (Exodus 19:8). The law that followed—the Ten Commandments and the whole structure of worship and community life—was not a ladder to climb but a charter for a people already rescued (Exodus 20:1-17). Notice the order: God did not say obey and I will deliver you, but I delivered you, therefore obey. The covenant carried both blessing for faithfulness and curse for rebellion (Deuteronomy 28:1-2; 28:15), and Israel's history would be the long record of both. The law revealed God's heart and humanity's failure, preparing the way for a covenant that would not depend on human resolve.

Application: Read the law as a gift that shows you your need, and let it drive you to grace rather than to self-effort.
Supporting references: Exodus 20:1-17; Exodus 24:1-8; Deuteronomy 28:1-2; Deuteronomy 28:15
The Covenant at MoabDeuteronomy 29:1-15 ↗

These are the words of the covenant that the LORD commanded Moses to make with the people of Israel in the land of Moab, besides the covenant that he had made with them at Horeb (Deuteronomy 29:1). This is a distinct, second covenant on the plains of Moab, just before Israel crossed the Jordan. Moses gathered the whole nation—heads, tribes, elders, officers, little ones, wives, and the sojourner—and recited to them the whole shape of the covenant: the blessings for obedience and the curses for rebellion, the call to choose life (Deuteronomy 30:19). But Moab is also the covenant where Moses foretold Israel's future failure and their eventual restoration: when all these things come upon you, the blessing and the curse, and you call them to mind among all the nations, and return to the LORD your God, then the LORD your God will restore your fortunes (Deuteronomy 30:1-3). The covenant at Moab is a renewal of Horeb for a new generation, and it is the first covenant that explicitly anticipates the need for a new covenant—a covenant God Himself will make to write His law on their hearts. Hebrews later quotes Moses' prophecy (Deuteronomy 32:35) as evidence of the need for something beyond Sinai (Hebrews 8:9).

Application: Choose life—and when you fall, remember that God's covenant anticipates restoration, not just judgment.
Supporting references: Deuteronomy 30:1-20; Jeremiah 31:32; Hebrews 8:9
The Covenant of the SabbathExodus 31:12-17 ↗

The LORD said to Moses: you are to speak to the people of Israel and say, above all you shall keep my Sabbaths, for this is a sign between me and you throughout your generations, that you may know that I, the LORD, sanctify you (Exodus 31:13). It is a sign forever, and a perpetual covenant (Exodus 31:16-17). Every seventh day, Israel stopped working—not because God needed rest, but because He wanted His people to know who sanctifies them. The Sabbath was a weekly, embodied covenant sign, distinct from circumcision (once) or the rainbow (rare). It was the recurring sign that the people belonged to God and that their life came from Him, not from their labor. The prophets later named the Sabbath as the covenant sign Israel most grieved: I gave them my Sabbaths, as a sign between me and them, that they might know that I am the LORD who sanctifies them (Ezekiel 20:12). Hebrews uses the Sabbath rest as a picture of the deeper rest that remains for the people of God in Christ (Hebrews 4:9-10). The Sabbath covenant is significant because it turns time itself into a covenant sign, teaching that God's people are defined not by what they do but by who sanctifies them.

Application: Let one day a week, at minimum, remind you that your life rests on God's work, not your own.
Supporting references: Exodus 20:8-11; Ezekiel 20:12, 20; Hebrews 4:9-10
The Covenant with PhinehasNumbers 25:10-13 ↗

When Israel sinned with the daughters of Moab and the men of Israel bowed down to Baal, God's anger burned, and Moses commanded the judges to execute those who had yoked themselves to Baal. In the middle of the plague, Phinehas, the son of Eleazar, stood up and acted with zeal, and the plague stopped (Numbers 25:7-9). The LORD spoke to Moses: Phinehas the son of Eleazar, son of Aaron the priest, has turned back my wrath from the people of Israel, in that he was jealous with my jealousy among them, so that I did not consume the people of Israel in my jealousy. Therefore say, Behold, I give to him my covenant of peace, and it shall be to him and to his descendants after him the covenant of a perpetual priesthood, because he was jealous for his God and made atonement for the people of Israel (Numbers 25:11-13). Phinehas's covenant was not earned by his own merit but granted in response to zeal for God's holiness. It is significant for two reasons: it shows that God honors a zeal for His holiness that is willing to act even when the culture around us does not, and it grants the priesthood a permanence that foreshadows the eternal priesthood of Christ, who offered Himself once for all (Hebrews 7:23-24).

Application: Be jealous for God's holiness—the covenant honors those who act, not those who merely grieve.
Supporting references: Psalm 106:30-31; 1 Chronicles 6:4-15; Hebrews 7:23-24
The Covenant of the GibeonitesJoshua 9:3-27 ↗

The Gibeonites lived in the land God had promised to Israel, and they knew they would be destroyed. So they sent a delegation to Joshua, wearing worn-out sacks and patched sandals, with dry, crumbly bread and old wineskins. They pretended to have come from a far country and asked Joshua to make a covenant with them (Joshua 9:3-15). Joshua and the leaders of Israel did not inquire of the LORD, and made peace with them and made a covenant, and the leaders of the congregation swore to them (Joshua 9:15). Three days later Israel learned the truth—the Gibeonites were their neighbors. The people grumbled, but the leaders said: we have sworn to them by the LORD, the God of Israel, and now we may not touch them (Joshua 9:19). The Gibeonites were spared and became woodcutters and water carriers for the house of God. Generations later, when Saul tried to wipe out the Gibeonites in his zeal for Israel, God sent a famine on Israel until David made atonement (2 Samuel 21:1-9). The significance is uncomfortable and clear: a covenant, once sworn before the LORD—even under deception—is binding on the ones who swore it. God takes human covenants seriously, which is one reason the Bible treats covenant fidelity as sacred, not casual.

Application: Be careful what you swear before the LORD—once made, a covenant binds you even when you were deceived.
Supporting references: 2 Samuel 21:1-9; Joshua 10:1-14
The Covenant with RahabJoshua 2:8-14 ↗

Rahab was a prostitute in Jericho, whose house was built into the city wall. When Joshua sent two spies into Jericho, she hid them on her roof and sent the king's men away (Joshua 2:1-7). Before they slept, she came up to them and said: I know that the LORD has given you the land, and that the fear of you has fallen upon us… for the LORD your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth beneath (Joshua 2:9-11). She asked them to swear by the LORD that they would deal kindly with her father's house, and she asked for a sign of good faith—that they would spare her father, mother, brothers, sisters, and all who belonged to them, and that they would save their lives from death (Joshua 2:12-13). The men answered: Our life for yours! If you do not tell this business of ours, then when the LORD gives us the land we will deal kindly and faithfully with you (Joshua 2:14). The word covenant is not used at this exact moment, but this is a sworn oath of rescue between a pagan and Israel, sealed with the scarlet cord in her window (Joshua 2:17-21). She is significant out of proportion to her story. She is the only person in Joshua who is saved by faith—Hebrews 11:31 lists her as a hero of faith; James 2:25 uses her as the example of faith shown in works; and she is grafted into the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). The oath of rescue at Jericho is the smallest covenant in Scripture and the one that puts a Moabite pagan into the family line of the Messiah.

Application: No past disqualifies you from God's covenant—Rahab's faith made her an ancestor of Christ.
Supporting references: Joshua 6:22-25; Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25; Matthew 1:5
The Covenant of David and Jonathan1 Samuel 18:1-4 ↗

After David killed Goliath, the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul (1 Samuel 18:1). That day Saul kept David in his household, and Jonathan made a covenant with David because he loved him as his own soul (1 Samuel 18:3). He stripped off the robe that was on him and gave it to David, along with his armor, his sword, his bow, and his belt (1 Samuel 18:4). Jonathan was the heir apparent—the covenant cost him something. Later, as Saul hunted David, Jonathan went to him in the wilderness and strengthened his hand in God, and said to him: Do not fear, for the hand of Saul my father shall not find you. You shall be king over Israel, and I shall be next to you (1 Samuel 23:16-17). The covenant of David and Jonathan is a personal covenant of loyalty sworn before the LORD (1 Samuel 20:16-17), in which one man who would be king and one man who would be next to him bound themselves to each other for life. Its significance is that it shows what covenant loyalty looks like when it costs—Jonathan chose faithfulness over advancement, and by his covenant with David he tied his own house to the house of the Messiah. Jesus said: greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends (John 15:13); Jonathan shows that love in advance, on the road to David's throne.

Application: Covenant loyalty is choosing faithfulness over advancement—commit to a friend even when it costs you.
Supporting references: 1 Samuel 20:16-17; 1 Samuel 23:15-18; 2 Samuel 1:26
The Davidic Covenant2 Samuel 7:1-17 ↗

David wanted to build God a house—a grand temple to replace the tent—but God turned the tables through the prophet Nathan (2 Samuel 7:1-7). God told David that instead He would build David a house, and that a descendant from David's line would reign forever (2 Samuel 7:12-16). David responded with a prayer of astonished humility, asking who he was that God had brought him this far (2 Samuel 7:18). The promise echoed through the Psalms and the prophets, sustaining Israel through exile and disappointment (Psalm 89:3-4), and it found its yes and amen in a child born in Bethlehem (Luke 1:32-33). When the angel told Mary her son would reign on David's throne forever, every syllable of this covenant was in view. The throne promised to David was not a temporary seat but an eternal one.

Application: Rest in the certainty that God's promises to David are fulfilled in Jesus, whose kingdom will never end.
Supporting references: 2 Samuel 7:18-29; Psalm 89:3-4; Luke 1:32-33
The Covenant of LeviMalachi 2:4-5 ↗

The book of Malachi opens with a charge against the priests. God says: So shall you know that I have sent this command to you, that my covenant with Levi may stand, says the LORD of hosts. My covenant with him was one of life and peace, and I gave them to him; it was a covenant of fear, and he feared me; he stood in awe of my name (Malachi 2:4-5). The Levites were set apart to serve the tabernacle and the temple, to carry the ark, to teach the people the law of God, and to make atonement at the altar. Malachi describes the covenant with Levi as one of life and peace, given out of God's initiative, kept through the priest's fear of God. But the priests of Malachi's day had corrupted the covenant. They had turned aside from the way, caused many to stumble by their instruction, and corrupted the covenant of Levi, says the LORD of hosts (Malachi 2:8). The significance of the covenant with Levi is that it was intended to preserve true worship and true teaching of the law, and it was broken when the priests stopped taking God seriously. It is a covenant of the sanctuary, and it shows how much God cares about the people who teach His word and lead His worship. Hebrews picks this up: if perfection had been attainable through the Levitical priesthood, why was there need for another priest after the order of Melchizedek? (Hebrews 7:11-14). The covenant of Levi points to the greater priesthood of Christ.

Application: The people who teach and lead worship carry a covenant—be faithful in the teaching and service you are given.
Supporting references: Malachi 2:6-9; Nehemiah 13:29; Numbers 25:12-13; Hebrews 7:11-14
The Covenant of MarriageMalachi 2:14 ↗

In Malachi's day, men were divorcing their wives to marry foreign women, and the prophet names it for what it is: you cover the LORD's altar with tears, with weeping and groaning because he no longer regards the offering or accepts it with favor from your hand. But you say, Why does he not? Because the LORD was witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been faithless, though she is your companion and your wife by covenant (Malachi 2:13-14). Proverbs calls marriage the covenant of her God (Proverbs 2:17). Scripture treats marriage not as a contract two people can dissolve at will but as a covenant made before God, in which God is the witness. Malachi says God hates divorce (Malachi 2:16) because covenant is sacred, and it is the same covenant loyalty that God shows to His people. Paul later uses marriage as a picture of Christ and the church—husbands, love your wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her (Ephesians 5:25-27). The covenant of marriage is significant because it is the most personal covenant in Scripture: it teaches that covenant faithfulness is not just a national or priestly theme but a daily, one-flesh, lifelong reality. It also explains why breaking it matters so much—it dishonors God's covenant, and it breaks an image of His covenant with His people.

Application: Treat your marriage, if you are married, as a covenant, not a contract—God is the witness.
Supporting references: Proverbs 2:17; Malachi 2:15-16; Ephesians 5:25-33
The New CovenantJeremiah 31:31-34 ↗

Through Jeremiah, God spoke of a day when He would make a new covenant—not like the one at Sinai, which the people broke (Jeremiah 31:31-32). This time the law would be written not on stone but on hearts, and He would be their God and they would be His people (Jeremiah 31:33). The most astonishing promise in it is the forgiveness: God declares He will forgive their wickedness and remember their sins no more (Jeremiah 31:34). On the night before He died, Jesus took a cup and said it was the new covenant in His blood, poured out for His disciples (Luke 22:20). The book of Hebrews explains why this covenant is superior—because it rests on a better sacrifice and a better priest, and because it actually does what the old one could only point toward (Hebrews 8:6-13). Every time you come to the Lord's Table, you are touching this covenant—heart, Spirit, forgiveness—sealed in blood and offered freely (Hebrews 10:16-18).

Application: Come to the Lord's Table with gratitude, knowing the new covenant is sealed in Christ's blood and secured forever.
Supporting references: Luke 22:20; Hebrews 8:6-13; Hebrews 10:16-18
