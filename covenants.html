A Note Before You Begin

This page covers the five major covenants that shape the Bible's storyline—Noahic, Abrahamic, Mosaic, Davidic, and New. Other covenants appear in Scripture (such as the covenant with Phinehas in Numbers 25), but these five form the central spine of redemption history. Each entry offers a brief narrative overview with key references for your own study. Read prayerfully, and let these notes be a doorway rather than a destination.

The Five Major Covenants

The Noahic CovenantGenesis 9:8-17 ↗

The world had become so violent that God looked upon it and grieved (Genesis 6:6). Into that ruin came a command to build an ark, and with it a promise that God had not abandoned His creation (Genesis 6:18). After the floodwaters receded and Noah stood on dry ground, God established a covenant not only with Noah but with every living creature (Genesis 9:12-13). The sign was a bow in the clouds—a rainbow—set in the sky as a reminder that God would never again destroy the earth by flood (Genesis 9:13-15). This first covenant is striking in its generosity: it binds God to a promise He will keep regardless of human faithfulness (Genesis 8:21-22). The rainbow is not a symbol of human goodness but of divine restraint. When you see one, you are looking at a pledge made before you were born.

Application: When you see a rainbow, pause and thank God for His covenant faithfulness that does not depend on your performance.
Supporting references: Genesis 6:18; Genesis 8:21-22; Genesis 9:12-13
The Abrahamic CovenantGenesis 15:1-21 ↗

God spoke to a man in a pagan city and told him to leave everything familiar for a land he had never seen, promising land, offspring, and blessing for all peoples on earth (Genesis 12:1-3). Abraham waited years without a son, until God took him outside and told him to count the stars (Genesis 15:5). When Abraham believed, it was credited to him as righteousness (Genesis 15:6), a verse Paul later built an entire gospel upon (Romans 4:3). The covenant was sealed with a solemn ceremony where God alone passed between the divided animals, binding Himself to the promise while Abraham slept (Genesis 15:17). Later God added the sign of circumcision (Genesis 17:10-11) and confirmed the covenant after the near-sacrifice of Isaac on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22:15-18). This covenant is the root from which the whole tree of redemption grows.

Application: Trust that God's promises rest on His faithfulness, not on your ability to earn them.
Supporting references: Genesis 12:1-3; Genesis 17:1-14; Genesis 22:15-18; Romans 4:3
The Mosaic CovenantExodus 19:3-8 ↗

Three months after Israel walked out of Egypt, they camped at a mountain that shook with thunder and smoke (Exodus 19:16-18). God offered Israel a covenant: if they obeyed His voice, they would be His treasured possession among all nations (Exodus 19:5-6). The people answered in unison that everything the LORD had said they would do (Exodus 19:8). The law that followed—the Ten Commandments and the whole structure of worship and community life—was not a ladder to climb but a charter for a people already rescued (Exodus 20:1-17). Notice the order: God did not say obey and I will deliver you, but I delivered you, therefore obey. The covenant carried both blessing for faithfulness and curse for rebellion (Deuteronomy 28:1-2; 28:15), and Israel's history would be the long record of both. The law revealed God's heart and humanity's failure, preparing the way for a covenant that would not depend on human resolve.

Application: Read the law as a gift that shows you your need, and let it drive you to grace rather than to self-effort.
Supporting references: Exodus 20:1-17; Exodus 24:1-8; Deuteronomy 28:1-2; Deuteronomy 28:15
The Davidic Covenant2 Samuel 7:1-17 ↗

David wanted to build God a house—a grand temple to replace the tent—but God turned the tables through the prophet Nathan (2 Samuel 7:1-7). God told David that instead He would build David a house, and that a descendant from David's line would reign forever (2 Samuel 7:12-16). David responded with a prayer of astonished humility, asking who he was that God had brought him this far (2 Samuel 7:18). The promise echoed through the Psalms and the prophets, sustaining Israel through exile and disappointment (Psalm 89:3-4), and it found its yes and amen in a child born in Bethlehem (Luke 1:32-33). When the angel told Mary her son would reign on David's throne forever, every syllable of this covenant was in view. The throne promised to David was not a temporary seat but an eternal one.

Application: Rest in the certainty that God's promises to David are fulfilled in Jesus, whose kingdom will never end.
Supporting references: 2 Samuel 7:18-29; Psalm 89:3-4; Luke 1:32-33
The New CovenantJeremiah 31:31-34 ↗

Through Jeremiah, God spoke of a day when He would make a new covenant—not like the one at Sinai, which the people broke (Jeremiah 31:31-32). This time the law would be written not on stone but on hearts, and He would be their God and they would be His people (Jeremiah 31:33). The most astonishing promise in it is the forgiveness: God declares He will forgive their wickedness and remember their sins no more (Jeremiah 31:34). On the night before He died, Jesus took a cup and said it was the new covenant in His blood, poured out for His disciples (Luke 22:20). The book of Hebrews explains why this covenant is superior—because it rests on a better sacrifice and a better priest, and because it actually does what the old one could only point toward (Hebrews 8:6-13). Every time you come to the Lord's Table, you are touching this covenant—heart, Spirit, forgiveness—sealed in blood and offered freely (Hebrews 10:16-18).

Application: Come to the Lord's Table with gratitude, knowing the new covenant is sealed in Christ's blood and secured forever.
Supporting references: Luke 22:20; Hebrews 8:6-13; Hebrews 10:16-18
