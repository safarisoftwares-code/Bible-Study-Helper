A Note Before You Begin

This page is a biblical study on marriage, family, singleness, and the hard cases of divorce and remarriage. It is not a marriage manual and not a position paper on disputed questions. It aims to trace what Scripture actually says about the design of marriage, the roles of husband and wife, the dignity of singleness, the raising of children, and the way the gospel reshapes every one of these. Read the verses in context. Where this page simplifies, Scripture does not. Bring the hard questions to your pastor and to God.

Marriage & Family — A Biblical Study

1. The Design of Marriage

Marriage as God's DesignGenesis 2:18-25 ↗

Marriage is not a human invention; it is God's design. In Genesis 2, God sees that it is not good for the man to be alone, and He says, I will make him a helper fit for him (Genesis 2:18). He forms the woman from the man's side—not from his head to rule over him, and not from his feet to be trampled by him, as Matthew Henry famously said, but from his side to be equal with him, under his arm to be protected, and near his heart to be loved. The man sees her and says, This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh (Genesis 2:23). The chapter ends with the first wedding: Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh (Genesis 2:24). Jesus quotes this exact verse in Matthew 19:5 when He is asked about divorce, treating Genesis 2:24 as the foundation of all marriage. Marriage is God's good design for companionship, for procreation, for mutual help, and for the display of covenant love.

Application: Thank God for the gift of marriage as His design—and if you are married, ask Him to help you cherish it.
Supporting references: Genesis 1:26-28; Matthew 19:4-6; Hebrews 13:4
The Two Become One FleshGenesis 2:24 ↗

Genesis 2:24 is the Bible's one-sentence definition of marriage: Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh. There are three movements here. First, leaving—a new identity is formed, a new household begins, and primary loyalty shifts from parents to spouse. Second, holding fast—the Hebrew word davak means to cling, to be glued together, to be joined inseparably. Third, one flesh—the union of body, soul, and life. Jesus quotes this verse in Matthew 19:5 and then adds a comment that is one of the most important sentences in Scripture on marriage: So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate (Matthew 19:6). The one-flesh union is not merely a physical act; it is the whole-life union of two people before God. And notice: it is God who joins. Marriage is not just a human contract between two willing parties; it is a divine act, and that is why it cannot be dissolved casually.

Application: If you are married, treat your marriage as a union God Himself has made—not a contract you can walk away from when it is hard.
Supporting references: Matthew 19:4-6; Mark 10:6-9; Ephesians 5:31-32
Marriage as a Picture of Christ and the ChurchEphesians 5:22-33 ↗

Paul says the deepest meaning of marriage is that it is a picture of Christ and the church. He writes, Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word, so that he might present the church to himself in splendor (Ephesians 5:25-27). And then he says, This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:32). Every Christian marriage is meant to be a living parable. The husband's sacrificial love pictures Christ's sacrificial love for His bride. The wife's respectful submission pictures the church's glad submission to her Lord. This is why marriage matters so much—not just for the couple, but for the gospel. A marriage that reflects Christ and the church is a testimony to a watching world. A marriage that distorts it is a distorting of the picture. This is why marriage is worth fighting for, and why the enemy attacks it so relentlessly.

Application: Ask yourself today: does my marriage make Christ and the church more believable, or less?
Supporting references: Revelation 19:6-9; Revelation 21:2; Isaiah 62:5
The Marriage CovenantMalachi 2:14-16 ↗

Scripture calls marriage a covenant, not merely a contract. Malachi rebukes the men of Israel for divorcing their wives: The LORD was witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been faithless, though she is your companion and your wife by covenant (Malachi 2:14). Proverbs describes the adulteress as one who forgets the covenant of her God (Proverbs 2:17). A contract is a transaction between two parties that can be dissolved by mutual agreement. A covenant is a sacred bond made before God, with God as witness, that is not to be broken. This is why God says, For I hate divorce, says the LORD, the God of Israel (Malachi 2:16). Not because He hates divorced people—He is full of compassion for the brokenhearted—but because He hates what divorce does to the covenant, to the couple, to the children, and to the picture of Christ and the church. Marriage is a covenant, and covenants are meant to be kept.

Application: Treat your marriage as a covenant made before God—not a contract you can renegotiate when you feel like it.
Supporting references: Proverbs 2:17; Matthew 19:6; Hebrews 13:4
Marriage Is HonorableHebrews 13:4 ↗

Hebrews gives one of the clearest positive statements about marriage in the New Testament: Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterous (Hebrews 13:4). Marriage is to be honored—publicly, culturally, and personally. The marriage bed is to be kept pure—which means both that sexual intimacy within marriage is good and holy, and that sexual intimacy outside marriage is not. The world tells us the opposite: that marriage is optional, that sex is a recreational activity, that commitment is a cage. The Bible says marriage is honorable—it is a gift of God, a picture of Christ, and a foundation of society. Paul tells the Corinthians that because of temptation, each man should have his own wife and each woman her own husband, and that they should not deprive each other except by agreement for a time, so that Satan may not tempt them (1 Corinthians 7:2-5). The marriage bed is a protection, a gift, and a picture of covenant love.

Application: Honor marriage this week—in how you speak about it, how you pray for it, and how you live it if you are married.
Supporting references: 1 Corinthians 7:1-5; Proverbs 5:15-19; 1 Timothy 4:1-5

2. Husbands and Wives

Husbands: Love as Christ LovedEphesians 5:25-30 ↗

Paul gives the husband a command that is staggering in its scope: Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her (Ephesians 5:25). The standard is not I love you when you are lovely or I love you when you deserve it—the standard is Christ's love for the church, which He demonstrated on the cross while we were still sinners. The husband is called to sacrificial love, sanctifying love, nourishing love, and cherishing love (Ephesians 5:25-29). Paul tells the Colossians, Husbands, love your wives, and do not be harsh with them (Colossians 3:19). Peter adds that husbands are to live with their wives in an understanding way, showing honor to the woman as the weaker vessel, since they are heirs with you of the grace of life, so that your prayers may not be hindered (1 Peter 3:7). This is not a love that is earned; it is a love that is given. And it is a love that is measured by the cross.

Application: Husbands, ask yourself this week: does my love for my wife look like Christ's love for the church—sacrificial, patient, and constant?
Supporting references: Colossians 3:19; 1 Peter 3:7; 1 Corinthians 13:4-7
Wives: Respect and SubmissionEphesians 5:22-24, 33 ↗

Paul writes, Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord. For the husband is the head of the wife even as Christ is the head of the church, his body, and is himself its Savior. Now as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands (Ephesians 5:22-24). And then, in his summary: Let each one of you love his wife as himself, and let the wife see that she respects her husband (Ephesians 5:33). The word submit—hypotasso in Greek—is a military word meaning to arrange oneself under. It is not a word of inferiority; it is a word of order. It is the same word used of Christ submitting to His parents (Luke 2:51) and of believers submitting to one another (Ephesians 5:21). Paul is not saying women are inferior; he is saying there is an order in marriage that reflects the order in the Trinity. Peter adds that wives of unbelieving husbands may win them without a word by their respectful conduct (1 Peter 3:1-2). And in all of this, the wife's worth is not measured by her submission; it is measured by her identity in Christ.

Application: Wives, ask God to help you respect your husband—not because he has earned it, but because Christ is honored by it.
Supporting references: 1 Peter 3:1-6; Colossians 3:18; Titus 2:3-5
Mutual SubmissionEphesians 5:21 ↗

The most important verse on submission in marriage is the one that gets quoted least: submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ (Ephesians 5:21). This verse is the header for everything that follows in Ephesians 5:22-6:9—wives and husbands, children and parents, servants and masters. All of it flows out of mutual submission. Paul tells the Philippians, Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility count others more significant than yourselves. Let each of you look not only to his own interests, but also to the interests of others (Philippians 2:3-4). And then he gives the greatest example: Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant (Philippians 2:5-7). Mutual submission is not a loss of identity; it is the shape of the gospel. And it makes the wife's submission in Ephesians 5:22-24 and the husband's love in Ephesians 5:25 understandable—both are expressions of the same gospel-shaped life.

Application: Practice mutual submission this week—look for one way to put your spouse's interests ahead of your own.
Supporting references: Philippians 2:3-4; Romans 12:10; 1 Peter 5:5
The Husband's Role as Head1 Corinthians 11:3 ↗

Paul writes, But I want you to understand that the head of every man is Christ, the head of a wife is her husband, and the head of Christ is God (1 Corinthians 11:3). Headship in Scripture is not tyranny; it is Christlike leadership. Christ is the head of the church, and He laid down His life for the church (Ephesians 5:25). The husband who is head of his wife is called to lead the way Christ leads—by serving, by sacrificing, by protecting, by nourishing. Paul goes on in 1 Corinthians 11:8-12 to insist that man and woman are not independent of each other—woman was made from man, but man is born of woman, and all things are from God. Headship is not a statement about superiority; it is a statement about order, responsibility, and Christlike love. The husband will give an account to God for how he led his family. And the whole thing is meant to picture Christ and the church.

Application: Husbands, if you lead, lead like Christ—by laying down your life, not by lording it over anyone.
Supporting references: Ephesians 5:23; 1 Corinthians 11:8-12; Genesis 2:18-24
The Wife's Role as HelperGenesis 2:18 ↗

The first word God says about the woman is that she is a helper fit for him (Genesis 2:18). The Hebrew word is ezer, which is also used of God Himself as Israel's helper (Psalm 121:2; Psalm 146:5). So being a helper is not a lower role—it is a divine role. The woman is a match for the man, a strength alongside him, a partner in the work God has given them together. Proverbs 31 paints a stunning portrait of a woman who fears the Lord—she rises before dawn, provides for her household, buys a field, plants a vineyard, cares for the poor, speaks with wisdom, and her husband praises her (Proverbs 31:10-31). She is not a wallflower; she is an active, industrious, wise partner in the household. The role of helper is not the role of doormat; it is the role of the one who comes alongside to strengthen, support, and complete.

Application: Wives, see your role as helper as God sees it—a divine calling, a strength alongside, a partner in the work of the Lord.
Supporting references: Proverbs 31:10-31; Genesis 2:20-25; 1 Corinthians 11:9
Communication and Conflict in MarriageEphesians 4:25-32 ↗

Paul gives the church in Ephesus a series of commands that are especially relevant to marriage: Therefore, having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor, for we are members one of another. Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil... Let no corrupting talk come out of your mouths, but only such as is good for building up... Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice. Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you (Ephesians 4:25-32). Proverbs says, A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger (Proverbs 15:1). James says, Know this, my beloved brothers: let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger (James 1:19). Conflict in marriage is inevitable—two sinners living in close quarters will clash. The question is not whether you will have conflict but how you will handle it: with truth and grace, with quick forgiveness, with soft answers, with quick listening.

Application: This week, practice one discipline of conflict: either listen more than you speak, or forgive faster than you are forgiven.
Supporting references: Proverbs 15:1; James 1:19; Colossians 3:12-15

3. Parenting and Family

Children Are a Gift from GodPsalm 127:3-5 ↗

The Psalmist writes, Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, the fruit of the womb a reward. Like arrows in the hand of a warrior are the children of one's youth. Blessed is the man who fills his quiver with them! (Psalm 127:3-5). Children are not a burden; they are a heritage and a reward. They are arrows in the hand of a warrior—which means they are meant to be aimed, directed, and released with purpose. The Bible also says, Grandchildren are the crown of the aged, and the glory of children is their fathers (Proverbs 17:6). Children are a gift of God, and the way we treat them reflects what we believe about the Giver. In a culture that treats children as an inconvenience—or as an accessory, or as a lifestyle choice—the Bible says they are a heritage and a reward. The way a society treats its children is a measure of its heart. And the way a family treats its children is a measure of its faith.

Application: If you have children, thank God for them this week—and ask Him to help you see them as gifts, not burdens.
Supporting references: Psalm 128:1-4; Proverbs 17:6; Genesis 33:5
Train Up a ChildProverbs 22:6 ↗

Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it (Proverbs 22:6). The Hebrew word for train is hanak, which means to dedicate, to initiate, to narrow the path. It is the same word used of dedicating a house (Deuteronomy 20:5) or the temple (1 Kings 8:63). Training a child is not just teaching facts; it is shaping a life. Moses tells Israel, You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might. And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise (Deuteronomy 6:5-7). Notice: not a scheduled hour of Bible study per week, but a constant, all-day, everyday conversation. Not just doctrine, but love. Not just rules, but relationship. Not just teaching, but life.

Application: Look for one natural moment today to talk with a child about God—not a forced lesson, just a conversation.
Supporting references: Deuteronomy 6:4-9; Ephesians 6:4; Proverbs 29:15
Fathers: Do Not Provoke Your ChildrenEphesians 6:4 ↗

Paul writes, Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord (Ephesians 6:4). Notice the two commands: do not provoke, and do bring up. A father can provoke his children in many ways—by harshness, by neglect, by favoritism, by rules without relationship, by criticism without encouragement, by absence. Paul tells the Colossians, Fathers, do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged (Colossians 3:21). Discouragement is the fruit of provocation. And then the positive command: bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord. The Greek word for discipline is paideia, which means training, instruction, correction—not punishment for its own sake, but the shaping of a child toward maturity. Hebrews says, For the moment all discipline seems painful rather than pleasant, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it (Hebrews 12:11). The father's job is not to crush the child but to shape him, not to punish out of anger but to train out of love.

Application: Fathers, ask yourself this week: does my child feel provoked or encouraged by me—and what do I need to change?
Supporting references: Colossians 3:21; Hebrews 12:5-11; Proverbs 13:24
Children: Honor Your ParentsEphesians 6:1-3 ↗

Paul writes, Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. Honor your father and mother (this is the first commandment with a promise), that it may go well with you and that you may live long in the land (Ephesians 6:1-3). He is quoting the fifth commandment (Exodus 20:12), which is the first command with a promise attached. The promise is that honoring parents leads to blessing—to a good life and long life. Honor is not the same as obedience. Obedience is for children; honor is for everyone, at every age. An adult child who no longer obeys their parents still honors them—by speaking of them well, by caring for them in their old age, by giving them a place at the table. Proverbs says, Hear, my son, your father's instruction, and forsake not your mother's teaching, for they are a graceful garland for your head and pendants for your neck (Proverbs 1:8-9). Obedience and honor are not a burden; they are a garland—something beautiful worn around the neck.

Application: If your parents are still living, find one concrete way to honor them this week—a call, a visit, a gift, or a word of thanks.
Supporting references: Exodus 20:12; Deuteronomy 5:16; Proverbs 1:8-9
The Family as a Small ChurchJoshua 24:15 ↗

Joshua gives one of the most famous statements of household faith: And if it is evil in your eyes to serve the LORD, choose this day whom you will serve, whether the gods your fathers served in the region beyond the River, or the gods of the Amorites in whose land you dwell. But as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD (Joshua 24:15). Notice that Joshua speaks for his household—not as a dictator, but as the leader who has already decided. The family is meant to be a small church, a place where worship, prayer, and the Word are lived out daily. Paul tells Timothy that an elder must manage his own household well, for if someone does not know how to manage his own household, how will he care for God's church? (1 Timothy 3:4-5). And when the Philippian jailer asked, What must I do to be saved? Paul answered, Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household (Acts 16:31). The family is meant to be a launching pad for the gospel, a place where children are raised, where hospitality is extended, and where the faith is passed on to the next generation.

Application: If you are a parent or a grandparent, make one specific plan this week for how your household will serve the LORD.
Supporting references: Acts 16:31-34; Deuteronomy 6:4-9; 1 Timothy 3:4-5
Family Worship and PrayerDeuteronomy 6:7 ↗

Moses tells Israel to teach the words of the LORD diligently to their children, and to talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise (Deuteronomy 6:7). Notice: family worship is not an event on Sunday morning; it is woven into the daily life of the family. The Psalmist says, We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD, and his might, and the wonders that he has done... so that they should set their hope in God and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments (Psalm 78:4-7). The goal of family worship is not information transfer; it is a transfer of hope—from one generation to the next. Paul saw this in Timothy: I am reminded of your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well (2 Timothy 1:5). The family is the first seminary. Parents are the first pastors. And family worship is the daily practice through which faith is passed on.

Application: If you have children in the home, start or strengthen one daily family habit this week—a short Bible reading, a prayer before bed, a conversation about God.
Supporting references: Psalm 78:1-8; Acts 2:42; 2 Timothy 1:5-6

4. Singleness, Divorce, and Hard Cases

Singleness as a Gift1 Corinthians 7:7-9 ↗

Paul writes, I wish that all were as I myself am. But each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another. To the unmarried and the widows I say that it is good for them to remain single, as I am. But if they cannot exercise self-control, they should marry. For it is better to marry than to burn with passion (1 Corinthians 7:7-9). Paul treats singleness not as a lesser state but as a gift—the word is charisma, the same word used for spiritual gifts. And he is not alone. Jesus says that some are eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven, and that whoever can receive this should receive it (Matthew 19:12). Isaiah promises that the eunuch who keeps God's covenant will receive a name better than sons and daughters (Isaiah 56:5). Singleness is a gift, not a curse. It is not a waiting room for marriage. It is a vocation, a calling, and a gift—for those who are called to it. And Paul adds a surprising reason: the unmarried person is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord, while the married person is anxious about worldly things, how to please his spouse (1 Corinthians 7:32-34). Singleness, in Paul's view, is an undistracted devotion to the Lord.

Application: If you are single, ask God to help you see your singleness as a gift and a calling, not a waiting room.
Supporting references: Matthew 19:11-12; 1 Corinthians 7:32-35; Isaiah 56:3-5
The Gift of Singleness1 Corinthians 7:32-35 ↗

Paul expands on the gift of singleness: I want you to be free from anxieties. The unmarried man is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord. But the married man is anxious about worldly things, how to please his wife, and his interests are divided. And the unmarried or betrothed woman is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to be holy in body and spirit. But the married woman is anxious about worldly things, how to please her husband. I say this for your own benefit, not to lay any restraint upon you, but to promote good order and to secure your undivided devotion to the Lord (1 Corinthians 7:32-35). Notice the phrase undivided devotion. Singleness is not just a state of not being married; it is a positive calling to undivided devotion to the Lord. Jesus lived this. Paul lived this. Many of the greatest missionaries and pastors have been single. Singleness is not a lesser existence; it is a different, good, God-given existence, with its own joys, its own challenges, and its own gift.

Application: If you are single, ask God to give you a vision for the unique, undivided devotion He is calling you to.
Supporting references: Matthew 19:11-12; Isaiah 56:3-5; Revelation 14:4
Sexuality Within Marriage1 Corinthians 7:3-5 ↗

Paul writes, The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband. For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does. Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement for a limited time, that you may devote yourselves to prayer; but then come together again, so that Satan may not tempt you because of your lack of self-control (1 Corinthians 7:3-5). Notice how positive and mutual this is. Sex in marriage is a gift of God, a right of both spouses, and a mutual responsibility. Proverbs paints a beautiful picture: Drink water from your own cistern, flowing water from your own well... Let your fountain be blessed, and rejoice in the wife of your youth, a lovely deer, a graceful doe. Let her breasts fill you at all times with delight; be intoxicated always in her love (Proverbs 5:15-19). The marriage bed is honorable, and the physical union of husband and wife is meant to be a source of joy, not shame. Sex is not the whole of marriage, but it is a real gift and a real part of it.

Application: If you are married, thank God for the gift of physical intimacy—and ask Him to help you cherish it as a gift, not neglect it as an optional extra.
Supporting references: Proverbs 5:15-19; Hebrews 13:4; Genesis 2:24-25
Sexuality Outside Marriage1 Corinthians 6:18-20 ↗

Paul writes, Flee from sexual immorality. Every other sin a person commits is outside the body, but the sexually immoral person sins against his own body. Or do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God? You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body (1 Corinthians 6:18-20). Notice the strong language: flee. Not negotiate, not dabble, not rationalize. Flee. Paul tells the Thessalonians, For this is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from sexual immorality; that each one of you know how to control his own body in holiness and honor, not in the passion of lust like the Gentiles who do not know God (1 Thessalonians 4:3-5). Hebrews says, Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterous (Hebrews 13:4). The biblical standard is clear: sex is for marriage—a one-flesh union between a husband and a wife, from which new life can come. This is not a prudish rule; it is a reflection of God's design and a protection for our own hearts.

Application: If you have been shaped by the world's view of sexuality, ask God to renew your mind with His Word this week.
Supporting references: 1 Thessalonians 4:3-5; Hebrews 13:4; Genesis 2:24
Divorce: What God HatesMalachi 2:14-16 ↗

Malachi is the clearest passage in the Old Testament on divorce: You cover the LORD's altar with tears, with weeping and groaning because he no longer regards the offering or accepts it with favor from your hand. But you say, Why does he not? Because the LORD was witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been faithless, though she is your companion and your wife by covenant. Did he not make them one, with a portion of the Spirit in their union? And what was the one God seeking? Godly offspring. So guard yourselves in your spirit, and let none of you be faithless to the wife of your youth. For the man who does not love his wife but divorces her, says the LORD, the God of Israel, covers his garment with violence, says the LORD of hosts. So guard yourselves in your spirit, and do not be faithless (Malachi 2:13-16). In the ESV, the phrase is rendered For the man who hates and divorces, but the underlying Hebrew is clear: God hates divorce. Not because He hates divorced people—He does not—but because divorce is violence against the covenant, against the spouse, against the children, and against the picture of Christ and the church. The prophet is addressing men who were divorcing their wives to marry foreign women, and God says: I see your tears, but I do not accept them, because your hands are stained with covenant-breaking.

Application: If your marriage is struggling, run to God before you run to a lawyer—He hates divorce, and He is able to restore.
Supporting references: Matthew 19:7-9; 1 Corinthians 7:10-11; Proverbs 6:16-19
Divorce: The Exception ClauseMatthew 19:9 ↗

Jesus is asked by the Pharisees whether it is lawful to divorce a wife for any cause (Matthew 19:3). His answer points back to Genesis 2:24 and then adds: What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate (Matthew 19:6). The Pharisees push back: Why then did Moses command one to give a certificate of divorce and to send her away? Jesus responds, Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce your wives, but from the beginning it was not so. And I say to you: whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery (Matthew 19:8-9). The exception clause—except for sexual immorality (Greek porneia)—is the ethical crux. Different Christian traditions read it differently. Some say it permits divorce in cases of adultery. Some say it permits divorce only in cases of sexual immorality and only as a last resort. Some say the exception clause applies only to the Jewish betrothal period, not to full marriage. The pastoral counsel here is not to settle the debate but to say: Scripture is clear that divorce is always tragic, but Scripture is also clear that in some cases it is permitted. God's grace is sufficient for those who have been divorced, and His gospel is sufficient for the restoration of broken lives.

Application: If you are wrestling with a hard marriage, do not settle the question on your own—bring it to a pastor who takes Scripture seriously.
Supporting references: Matthew 5:31-32; 1 Corinthians 7:15; Malachi 2:16
Remarriage: The Pastoral Question1 Corinthians 7:39 ↗

Paul writes, A wife is bound to her husband as long as he lives. But if her husband dies, she is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord (1 Corinthians 7:39). The plain teaching is that remarriage after the death of a spouse is permitted—and Paul says it is good. When it comes to remarriage after divorce, the Bible is less explicit, and different Christian traditions read the passages differently. Deuteronomy 24:1-4 describes a case in which a man divorces his wife, she marries another man, and the second husband divorces her or dies. In that case, the first husband may not take her back, because she has been defiled. That passage is descriptive, not prescriptive—it is describing what Israel did, not commanding it. The New Testament parallel is in 1 Corinthians 7:10-16, where Paul says that if an unbelieving spouse departs, the believing spouse is not enslaved—God has called us to peace. The pastoral question of remarriage after divorce is best answered by a pastor who knows the person, the circumstances, and the Scriptures, not by a rule applied from a distance.

Application: If you have been divorced, do not let the complexity of these passages keep you from seeking godly counsel—the gospel is bigger than your past.
Supporting references: Romans 7:1-3; Deuteronomy 24:1-4; Matthew 19:9
Restoration After Broken MarriagePsalm 34:18 ↗

The Psalmist writes, The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit (Psalm 34:18). The Bible is not naive about broken marriages. It does not pretend divorce does not happen, and it does not pretend it does not hurt. But the Bible is also full of promises for those who have been broken by it. Joel says, I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten (Joel 2:25). Jeremiah says, Return, faithless Israel, declares the LORD. I will not look on you in anger, for I am merciful, declares the LORD; I will not be angry forever (Jeremiah 3:12). John tells the story of the woman caught in adultery: Jesus says, Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more (John 8:11). The God of the Bible is not a God of second chances only; He is the God of the hundredth chance, the thousandth chance, the last chance. Restoration is not a slogan; it is the gospel. And the church is meant to be the community where that restoration is lived out.

Application: If you have been broken by divorce or by a spouse's sin, bring your grief to the God who is near to the brokenhearted—and let Him restore.
Supporting references: Joel 2:25; John 8:1-11; Jeremiah 3:12-14
