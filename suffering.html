A Note Before You Begin

This page is a biblical study on suffering, grief, and loss. It does not pretend that pain is simple or that every question has an easy answer. It walks through what Scripture actually says: that suffering is real, that God is present in it, that lament is holy, that grief has a shape, and that the gospel speaks into every kind of loss. Read slowly. Sit with the verses. Bring your own pain to this page, and let the Word meet you there.

Suffering, Grief & Loss — A Biblical Study

1. The Reality of Suffering

Suffering Is Real and ExpectedJohn 16:33 ↗

Jesus does not promise His followers an easy life. He promises them Himself. On the night before His death, He says, I have said these things to you, that in me you may have peace. In the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world (John 16:33). Notice: you will have tribulation. Not you might. Not if things go wrong. You will. Paul tells Timothy, all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted (2 Timothy 3:12). Peter tells the church not to be surprised at the fiery trial when it comes upon you to test you, as though something strange were happening to you (1 Peter 4:12). This is one of the most freeing truths in Scripture: suffering is not a sign that God has abandoned you or that your faith is weak. It is part of life in a fallen world, and Jesus has overcome the world.

Application: Stop being surprised by suffering—expect it, and trust the One who has overcome the world.
Supporting references: 2 Timothy 3:12; 1 Peter 4:12-13; Acts 14:22
The Fall and the CurseGenesis 3:14-19 ↗

Suffering entered the world through sin. In Genesis 2:16-17, God warned Adam that eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil would lead to death. When Adam and Eve disobeyed, the curse fell. The ground was cursed, work became toil, and the woman's pain in childbirth was multiplied (Genesis 3:16-19). Paul explains the cosmic scope of this: For the creation was subjected to futility, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it, in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now (Romans 8:20-22). All suffering traces back to the fall—both the original sin and the ongoing sin of a broken world. This means suffering is not what God intended; it is what sin produced. And it will not last forever.

Application: Remember that suffering is a symptom of a broken world—it is temporary, and God has promised to end it.
Supporting references: Romans 8:20-22; Genesis 2:16-17; Revelation 21:3-4
The Mystery of SufferingRomans 11:33-36 ↗

Not all suffering is explained. Paul ends the book of Romans with a doxology: Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways! For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor? Or who has given a gift to him that he might be repaid? For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be glory forever. Amen (Romans 11:33-36). God's ways are not our ways. Isaiah says, For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, declares the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways and my thoughts than your thoughts (Isaiah 55:8-9). Moses tells Israel, The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever (Deuteronomy 29:29). Job wrestles with God for chapters, and when God finally speaks, Job repents in dust and ashes (Job 42:1-6). The mystery of suffering is not a puzzle we solve; it is a Person we trust. We do not always know why, but we always know Who.

Application: When you cannot understand why, choose to trust the One who knows—even when He does not explain.
Supporting references: Isaiah 55:8-9; Job 42:1-6; Deuteronomy 29:29
God Is Near to the BrokenheartedPsalm 34:18 ↗

The Psalmist writes, The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit (Psalm 34:18). This is one of the most tender verses in the Bible. God is not distant from pain; He is present in it. Psalm 147:3 says, He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds. Isaiah says that God dwells in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit, to revive the spirit of the lowly, and to revive the heart of the contrite (Isaiah 57:15). Jesus says, Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted (Matthew 5:4). Notice the paradox: God dwells in the highest and holiest place, but He is also near to the lowest and most broken place. He is not embarrassed by your tears. He is not frustrated by your questions. He is not distant from your pain. He is near. The God of the universe moves toward the brokenhearted, not away from them.

Application: If you are brokenhearted today, know that God is not far off—He is near, and He hears.
Supporting references: Psalm 147:3; Isaiah 57:15; Matthew 5:4

2. Lament: The Bible's Language for Grief

Lament Is HolyPsalm 13 ↗

The psalms of lament are some of the most honest prayers in the Bible. Psalm 13 begins, How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me? (Psalm 13:1). Psalm 22 begins, My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? (Psalm 22:1)—the words Jesus quotes from the cross. Psalm 42: How long must I take counsel in my soul and have sorrow in my heart all the day? (Psalm 42:9). Psalm 88 ends without resolution: You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness (Psalm 88:18). Lamentations 3:1: I am the man who has seen affliction (Lamentations 3:1). And yet the Bible includes these laments in its pages. This means lament is holy. You do not have to be polished to pray. You do not have to be cheerful. You do not have to have everything figured out. The Bible gives you words for your anger, your confusion, your grief, and your questions—and it calls them prayer.

Application: Give yourself permission to lament—it is a form of prayer, not a failure of faith.
Supporting references: Psalm 22; Psalm 42; Psalm 88; Lamentations 3
Crying Out to GodPsalm 62:8 ↗

The Psalmist writes, Trust in him at all times, O people; pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us (Psalm 62:8). Pour out your heart—not a measured, edited, carefully-worded prayer, but a pouring. Hannah poured out her soul before the LORD at Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:15). David cried out, This poor man cried, and the LORD heard him and saved him out of all his troubles (Psalm 34:6). The people of Israel cried out in their slavery, and God heard their groaning (Exodus 2:23-25). Jesus Himself prayed with loud cries and tears in the days of His flesh (Hebrews 5:7). There is something about crying out that God welcomes and hears. Crying out is not a lack of faith; it is faith in the God who hears. The Psalms are full of people crying out—raw, unfiltered, sometimes angry, sometimes broken. And every cry is treated as a prayer.

Application: Pour out your heart to God this week—no filters, no editing, just honesty.
Supporting references: Psalm 34:6; Psalm 107:13; 1 Samuel 1:15-16
Honest Emotions Are Not SinPsalm 88 ↗

Psalm 88 is the darkest psalm in the Bible. It begins, O LORD, God of my salvation; I cry out day and night before you (Psalm 88:1). And it ends without a single ray of light: You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness (Psalm 88:18). No resolution, no thanksgiving, no praise at the end. And yet it is in the Bible. Job curses the day of his birth (Job 3:1). Jeremiah says, Cursed be the day on which I was born! (Jeremiah 20:14). Jesus says in Gethsemane, My soul is very sorrowful, even to death (Mark 14:34). And then He prays, Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will (Mark 14:36). Honest emotions are not sin. The Bible does not sanitize the grief of its heroes; it lets them speak. This means you can speak too.

Application: God is not threatened by your darkest thoughts—bring them to Him, and let Him hold them.
Supporting references: Job 3:1-26; Jeremiah 20:14-18; Mark 14:33-36
When God Is SilentPsalm 22:1-2 ↗

Psalm 22:1-2: My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, from the words of my groaning? O my God, I cry by day, but you do not answer, and by night, but I find no rest. Jesus quoted these words from the cross (Matthew 27:46). The Son of God Himself experienced the silence of the Father. Habakkuk begins, O LORD, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear? (Habakkuk 1:2). Lamentations describes the silence: He has walled me about so that I cannot escape; he has made my chains heavy (Lamentations 3:7). The silence of God is one of the hardest experiences in the Christian life. But the Bible does not pretend it does not happen. It gives voice to the silence, and it holds on to the God who is silent. Even when God is silent, He has not left. Even when He does not answer, He is still there.

Application: When God seems silent, keep speaking to Him—the Psalms show us how to keep talking when we cannot hear His answer.
Supporting references: Habakkuk 1:1-4; Lamentations 3:7-9; Matthew 27:46

3. The Shape of Grief

Grief Has Its Own TimelineEcclesiastes 3:1-8 ↗

Ecclesiastes says, For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven: a time to be born, and a time to die... a time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance (Ecclesiastes 3:1-4). Grief has its own timeline. It does not fit neatly into our schedules. Psalm 30:5 says, Weeping may tarry for the night, but joy comes with the morning. But it does not say how long the night will be. Psalm 126:5-6 says, Those who sow in tears shall reap with shouts of joy! He who goes out weeping, bearing the seed for sowing, shall come home with shouts of joy, bringing his sheaves with him. The sowing in tears comes before the reaping in joy. Grief is a season, not an event. It takes time. It comes in waves. It is unpredictable. And the promise of Scripture is not that you will not grieve, but that grief will not last forever—because one day, God will wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4).

Application: Let grief take the time it needs—do not rush it, and do not apologize for it.
Supporting references: Psalm 30:5; Psalm 126:5-6; Revelation 21:4
The Comfort of God2 Corinthians 1:3-5 ↗

Paul writes, Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and God of all comfort, who comforts us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction, with the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted by God. For as we share abundantly in Christ's sufferings, so through Christ we share abundantly in comfort too (2 Corinthians 1:3-5). The Greek word for comfort is paraklesis, which means to come alongside. The same word is used of the Holy Spirit, the Comforter, the Paraclete. God does not comfort us from a distance; He comes alongside us. Isaiah says, Comfort, comfort my people, says your God (Isaiah 40:1). Isaiah compares God's comfort to a mother's: As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you (Isaiah 66:13). And the Shepherd of Psalm 23 walks with us through the valley of the shadow of death (Psalm 23:4). The comfort of God is not the removal of pain; it is the presence of God in the pain.

Application: Do not face your grief alone—let God come alongside you, and let His people come alongside you too.
Supporting references: Isaiah 40:1; Isaiah 66:13; Psalm 23:4
The Fellowship of SufferingPhilippians 3:10 ↗

Paul writes, that I may know him and the power of his resurrection, and may share his sufferings, becoming like him in his death (Philippians 3:10). Notice the progression: knowing Christ, the power of His resurrection, sharing His sufferings, becoming like Him in His death. Suffering is not an obstacle to knowing Christ; it is one of the ways we come to know Him. Paul tells the Romans that we are children of God, and if children, then heirs—heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ, provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him (Romans 8:17). Peter says, Rejoice insofar as you share Christ's sufferings, that you may also rejoice and be glad when his glory is revealed (1 Peter 4:13). And Paul says, I rejoice in my sufferings for your sake, and in my flesh I am filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the church (Colossians 1:24). We are united to Christ in His death and in His resurrection, and we are also united to Him in His sufferings. That union is real, and it is precious.

Application: When you suffer, remember that you are not alone—Christ suffers with you, and you are united to Him in it.
Supporting references: Romans 8:17; 1 Peter 4:13; Colossians 1:24
Groaning with HopeRomans 8:18-25 ↗

Paul writes, For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us. For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God... And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies. For in this hope we were saved (Romans 8:18-24). Notice the word groan. Paul does not say we skip over suffering; he says we groan in it—but we groan with hope. We know the ending. 2 Corinthians 4:17 says, For this light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison. Peter says that our faith, though tested by fire, will result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ (1 Peter 1:7). The Christian can grieve with hope because we know that the story is not over. Revelation 21:4 promises that one day God will wipe away every tear from our eyes, and death shall be no more. That is what we groan toward.

Application: Grieve with hope—not the hope that skips suffering, but the hope that looks through it to the glory that is coming.
Supporting references: 2 Corinthians 4:16-18; 1 Peter 1:6-7; Revelation 21:4

4. Walking Through Suffering

God's Sufficient Grace2 Corinthians 12:7-10 ↗

Paul describes a thorn given to him in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to harass him, to keep him from becoming conceited. Three times he pleaded with the Lord about this, that it should leave him. But God said to him, My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:7-9). Paul does not name the thorn—which means every one of us can pour our own thorn into the text. And Paul's response is remarkable: Therefore I will boast all the more gladly of my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may rest upon me. For the sake of Christ, then, I am content with weaknesses, insults, hardships, persecutions, and calamities. For when I am weak, then I am strong (2 Corinthians 12:9-10). The grace of God does not always remove the thorn. But it is sufficient for the thorn. It is enough.

Application: If God has not removed your thorn, ask Him for the grace that is sufficient—because it is.
Supporting references: 2 Corinthians 11:23-28; Philippians 4:13; Hebrews 4:16
The God Who Weeps with UsJohn 11:33-37 ↗

When Lazarus died, Jesus went to the tomb. He saw Mary weeping, and the Jews who had come with her weeping, and He was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled (John 11:33). And then the shortest and most powerful verse in the New Testament: Jesus wept (John 11:35). Notice what makes this astonishing. Jesus knew that in a few minutes He would raise Lazarus from the dead. He knew that the story was not over. And yet He wept. He wept with the sisters. He wept in grief. He wept even though He knew the ending. And He weeps with us too. Isaiah calls Him a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief (Isaiah 53:3). Hebrews says, For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses (Hebrews 4:15). When you weep, Jesus weeps with you. Not because He does not know the end; but because He loves you in the middle.

Application: When you weep, remember that Jesus weeps with you—the God of the universe grieves alongside you.
Supporting references: Isaiah 53:3; Hebrews 4:15; Romans 12:15
The Comfort of HeavenRevelation 21:1-5 ↗

Revelation gives us the final picture: Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away, and the sea was no more. And I saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband. And I heard a loud voice from the throne saying, Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God. He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away. And he who was seated on the throne said, Behold, I am making all things new (Revelation 21:1-5). This is the goal of the story. Revelation 7:17 promises that the Lamb in the midst of the throne will be their shepherd, and he will guide them to springs of living water, and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes. Isaiah says that death will be swallowed up forever (Isaiah 25:8). Paul says that death will be swallowed up in victory (1 Corinthians 15:54). Heaven is the answer to every question suffering has asked.

Application: Fix your eyes on the promise of heaven—every tear wiped away, every grief ended, every pain healed.
Supporting references: Revelation 7:16-17; 1 Corinthians 15:54-57; Isaiah 25:8
Suffering Is Not Worth ComparingRomans 8:18 ↗

Romans 8:18 says, For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us. This is not a promise that our suffering is small; it is a promise that the glory is bigger. 2 Corinthians 4:16-18 says, So we do not lose heart. Though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day. For this light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison, as we look not to the things that are seen but to the things that are unseen. For the things that are seen are transient, but the things that are unseen are eternal. Notice: light, momentary. And yet Paul is not minimizing our suffering; he is maximizing the glory. This affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison. The word weight is the Greek word baros, which means a burden—but here it is a burden of glory. Suffering is real. But glory is realer, and it is coming.

Application: When suffering feels overwhelming, remember that it is preparing for you a weight of glory that will make it look light.
Supporting references: 2 Corinthians 4:16-18; 1 Peter 5:10; 2 Corinthians 4:17
God's Purpose in SufferingGenesis 50:20 ↗

Joseph says to his brothers, who had sold him into slavery and left him for dead: As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today (Genesis 50:20). This is one of the most important verses in the Bible about suffering. Notice: Joseph does not say that the brothers' evil was good. He says that God meant it for good. The evil was real; the good was real; and God was at work in both. Romans 8:28 says, And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose. Notice: all things. Not just the good things, not just the easy things—all things. God's purpose is not to make you comfortable; His purpose is to make you like Christ (Romans 8:29). And even the worst things can serve that purpose. God is not the author of evil, but He is the sovereign over it, and He can weave even the worst things into a good design.

Application: Trust that God is at work even in what is meant for evil—His purposes are good.
Supporting references: Romans 8:28; Jeremiah 29:11; 2 Corinthians 4:17
Suffering Produces CharacterRomans 5:3-5 ↗

Paul writes, Not only that, but we rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope, and hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us (Romans 5:3-5). Notice the chain: suffering produces endurance, endurance produces character, character produces hope. Suffering is not an obstacle to the Christian life; it is part of how the Christian life is formed. James says, Count it all joy, my brothers, when you meet trials of various kinds, for you know that the testing of your faith produces steadfastness (James 1:2-3). Peter says that the tested genuineness of your faith—more precious than gold that perishes though it is tested by fire—may be found to result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ (1 Peter 1:7). Hebrews says that God disciplines His children for their good, so that they might share in His holiness (Hebrews 12:10). Suffering is not the point; it is the tool.

Application: Ask God to produce in you endurance, character, and hope—even if it takes suffering to do it.
Supporting references: James 1:2-4; 1 Peter 1:6-7; Hebrews 12:10-11
